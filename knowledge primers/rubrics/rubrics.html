1 · What a rubric is: criteria, scales, weights and a decision rule

A rubric turns a quality judgement into a list of checkable statements, each with a scale, a weight and a rule for combining them. It is the contract between whoever wants the data, whoever produces it, and whoever scores it — a human rater, a model judge, or both. Everything else on this page is the same object in a different coat: annotation guidelines, judge prompts, reward functions, agent evals, acceptance criteria.

Where rubrics live: the verifiability spectrum

Some outputs can be checked exactly (a number, an exact string), some by a program (unit tests, a database state), and some only by judgement. Rubrics are the tool for the judgement end: they decompose a holistic verdict into criteria that are each closer to checkable. The further right you go, the more you cover and the less objective each check is — and the more you need the validation on card 3.

Scales. Binary is the most reliable and the easiest to validate; XpertBench, Toloka’s reasoning rubrics, RLCF checklists and ADK’s rubric criteria are all yes/no. Ordinal scales (1–5) are needed when degree matters — clarity, depth — and only work with a behavioural anchor and an example per level. Nominal scales are for reason codes and categories. Continuous scores from a judge (“rate 0–100”) look precise and are not; convert them to anchored levels or use them only as a smooth training signal.

Scope. A global rubric applies to every item (safety, tone, format, no fabricated citations). An instance-specific rubric is written for one prompt (“mentions that ibuprofen is contraindicated with the patient’s anticoagulant”). The strong 2025 benchmarks are instance-specific — HealthBench averages about ten physician-written criteria per conversation, XpertBench about seventeen checkpoints per task — and Vertex AI’s adaptive rubrics generate instance-specific criteria per prompt. Most real systems use both: a global layer plus per-item criteria.

Reference answers inform criteria; they are not criteria. Toloka’s reasoning datasets include an expert answer to help the judge but score against yes/no criteria so that valid alternative answers are not penalised. SWE-bench Verified exists because 61% of the original tasks had tests that could reject reasonable solutions — a reference used as the rubric.

Choosing the form before writing a word

Five questions decide the shape of the rubric. Answer them in this order and the type, scale and size follow.

  1. What decision does the score drive? Accept/reject an item (gates + checklist), rank systems (analytic with CIs), train a model (smooth, weighted, hack-resistant), give feedback to an author (single-point or analytic with evidence).
  2. Who scores? Humans only (fewer items, plain language, examples per level), a model only (self-contained items, evidence required, validated against humans first), both (binary items, identical wording).
  3. What can be verified by a program? Move it out of the rubric: tests, schema checks, word counts, forbidden strings. The rubric holds what only judgement can check.
  4. Is quality prompt-specific? If the right answer depends on the prompt (medicine, law, STEM, code), you need instance-specific criteria plus a global layer; if not (tone, format, safety), a global rubric is enough.
  5. How much time per item exists? A binary criterion with evidence costs a trained human roughly 5–10 seconds; twenty items is a two- to three-minute task. A model judge costs one call per item (or per criterion if you split them). Size the rubric to the budget, then cut the criteria that never discriminate (card 3).

Where rubrics go wrong at the design stage

  • Holistic only. “Rate 1–10” gives agreement in the 0.3–0.5 range and no diagnosis. MT-Bench-style scores are fine for leaderboards and useless for fixing a dataset.
  • The reference answer as the rubric. Valid alternatives get penalised; the judge becomes a similarity metric. Use the reference to derive criteria, then judge against the criteria.
  • Inferential statements. “Understands the user’s intent”, “shows expertise” — nobody can point to the span that proves it. Rewrite as what the response does.
  • Double-barrelled items. “Accurate and complete” is two criteria with one verdict; agreement collapses on the half that differs.
  • Unweighted checklists. Every item worth one point means the longest answer wins; add tiers and pitfalls, or the rubric trains verbosity.
  • Five levels without anchors. Raters drift to 3 and 4; the scale carries one bit of information at five times the cost.
  • No decision rule. A rubric without “how the points become accept/reject/rank” is a questionnaire. Write the rule (card 8) before the pilot.
  • Continuous judge scores. “Give a score from 0 to 100” reports false precision; the same judge gives 73 and 81 to the same text on two runs.

from dataclasses import dataclass

@dataclass
class Verdict:
    item_id: str
    value: float          # binary: 1.0 met / 0.0 unmet; ordinal: level 1..L; None = cannot assess
    evidence: str

def score(rubric: dict, verdicts: dict[str, Verdict]) -> dict:
    items = {i["id"]: i for i in rubric["items"]}
    # 1 · gates: any failed gate ends it
    for i in items.values():
        if i.get("gate") and verdicts[i["id"]].value != 1.0:
            return {"score": 0.0, "accepted": False, "failed_gate": i["id"]}
    # 2 · weighted sum over positive and negative items, normalised by the maximum positive points
    total, max_pos, by_dim = 0.0, 0.0, {}
    for i in items.values():
        if i.get("gate"): continue
        v = verdicts[i["id"]].value
        if i["type"] == "ordinal":
            v = None if v is None else (v - 1) / (i["levels"] - 1)        # 1..L → 0..1
        w = i["weight"]
        if w > 0: max_pos += w
        if v is None: continue                                          # CANNOT_ASSESS → skip (policy: SKIP)
        contribution = w * v
        total += contribution
        by_dim[i["dimension"]] = by_dim.get(i["dimension"], 0.0) + contribution
    s = max(0.0, min(1.0, total / max_pos))                                 # HealthBench-style: penalties can pull below 0; clip
    return {"score": round(s, 3), "accepted": s >= rubric["decision"]["accept_at"], "by_dimension": by_dim}

Keep the rubric in a file with a version, and the scoring rule in code with a test. When a criterion changes, the version changes, and card 10 tells you what to re-score.

2 · Writing a rubric: from a vague wish to checkable statements

Good rubrics are not written from the spec alone. They are derived from a reference answer and from candidate responses of varying quality: the criteria are whatever separates the best candidate from the worst, stated so that a stranger can check each one in seconds. Experts refine; models draft; the pilot decides.

intent · reference answer · 3–5 candidates of varying quality · failure modes

The drafting pipeline

The principles, as a checklist for each criterion

  • Observable. Describes what the response does or contains, not what the author knows or intends. “States that…”, “includes a…”, “does not…”.
  • Atomic. One thing per item. If you can imagine a response that half-meets it, split it.
  • Binary if possible. MET/UNMET with evidence. Use an ordinal scale only where the degree matters, and then anchor every level with a behaviour and an example.
  • Self-contained. The judge needs no outside knowledge: put the fact in the statement (“states the deadline is 30 June”) or in a supplied context field. RaR calls this self-containment; it is what lets small judge models match large ones.
  • Weighted by consequence. Tiers (Essential / Important / Optional) or points; gates for must-pass; negative pitfalls for the errors that look fine superficially.
  • Covers the outcome space. For every way the worst candidate is worse than the best, there is an item. For every way two good candidates differ, there is an item or a deliberate decision that the difference does not matter.
  • Length-neutral. Nothing in the rubric is satisfied by saying more; pitfalls catch padding, hedging and disclaimers.
  • Plain language, defined terms. No “appropriately”, “adequately”, “sufficient” without a definition; no double negatives.
  • Edge-case rules. A decision rule for the ambiguous cases the pilot surfaces (“if the response refuses for safety, items R-01…R-03 are NOT_APPLICABLE and the gate G-03 applies”).
  • Versioned with provenance. Who wrote it, from which reference, which pilot agreement it achieved, what changed since.

Writing one rubric, step by step

  1. Write the intent in one sentence with the decision it serves: “Accept solutions for the reasoning dataset only if a grader could not tell them from an expert’s.”
  2. Get a reference answer from an expert, and have the expert list what a weaker answer typically gets wrong.
  3. Generate four candidates at different quality levels (small and large models, or a human draft and a degraded copy). Score them intuitively first: best to worst.
  4. List the differences between adjacent candidates. Each difference that matters becomes a criterion; each that does not is written down as deliberately ignored.
  5. Draft the items (a model can do this well from steps 2–4) and rewrite each to be observable, atomic and self-contained.
  6. Classify: gates, tiers, pitfalls; assign weights; map each item to a dimension.
  7. Lint: no “and” inside an item, no undefined adverbs, no item satisfied by length, every ordinal level anchored with an example.
  8. Write the decision rule and the edge-case rules (refusals, partial answers, off-topic).
  9. Pilot on 30–50 items with three raters (card 3); compute agreement per criterion; revise.
  10. Freeze v1.0 with provenance; put it under version control; schedule the first drift check (card 10).

Sizing: XpertBench averages about 17 checkpoints per task with a 1–10 weight and a tier each; HealthBench about ten criteria per conversation on a −10…+10 scale; RaR rubrics run 7–20 items. Fewer than six items usually means the rubric is holistic in disguise; more than twenty usually means it has not been pruned by the pilot.

Pitfalls in the writing

  • Writing after seeing the outputs you want to pass. The rubric overfits one system; candidates from several sources and a blind pilot prevent it.
  • Copying the reference answer into criteria. “Mentions X, Y, Z” for every sentence of the reference turns the rubric into a recall metric that penalises better answers.
  • Criteria only an expert can judge. “The dosage is correct” forces the judge to know the dosage; “states a dose of 500 mg twice daily” does not.
  • No pitfalls. A rubric with only positive items is satisfied by a long hedge; HealthBench’s negative criteria and RaR’s pitfall items exist for this reason.
  • Forty items. Humans stop reading at twenty; judges split across calls lose context. Prune by discrimination after the pilot.
  • Unanchored degrees. “Depth: 1–5” measures the rater’s mood.
  • Missing the refusal case. The best response to a harmful prompt is a refusal that scores zero on every positive item; the rubric needs a rule for it.
  • One author, no review. XpertBench had every rubric reviewed by a second expert and spot-checked a third of them by seniors; Toloka routes prompts through editors. One expert’s blind spot becomes the dataset’s.

from typing import Literal

# lint_rubric.py — the mechanical half of review; the expert does the other half
import re
VAGUE = re.compile(r"\b(appropriate(ly)?|adequate(ly)?|sufficient(ly)?|good|well|properly|clearly|relevant|reasonable)\b", re.I)
INFER = re.compile(r"\b(understands?|knows?|demonstrates?|shows? (expertise|understanding)|intends?)\b", re.I)
LENGTH = re.compile(r"\b(detailed|thorough|comprehensive|in[- ]depth|extensive)\b", re.I)

def lint(items: list[Item]) -> list[str]:
    problems = []
    for it in items:
        t = it.text
        if " and " in t and it.polarity == "positive": problems.append(f"{it.id}: double-barrelled ('and') — split")
        if VAGUE.search(t):  problems.append(f"{it.id}: undefined qualifier — state the fact or the threshold")
        if INFER.search(t):  problems.append(f"{it.id}: inferential — rewrite as what the response does")
        if LENGTH.search(t): problems.append(f"{it.id}: rewards length — replace with the specific content expected")
        if len(t.split()) > 30: problems.append(f"{it.id}: too long to judge in one pass")
    if not any(i.polarity == "pitfall" for i in items): problems.append("no pitfall items — verbosity will win")
    if not any(i.tier == "essential" for i in items): problems.append("no essential items — nothing is gating")
    return problems

3 · Validating a rubric: does it measure the same thing twice, and does it measure the right thing?

A rubric is an instrument, and instruments are calibrated. Reliability asks whether two raters — or two runs of a judge — give the same verdict. Validity asks whether the verdict tracks what you actually care about. Discrimination asks whether a criterion ever separates items at all. Measure all three per criterion, not for the rubric as a whole, and fix the items that fail.

Validity: three questions no agreement statistic answers

  • Criterion validity. Do rubric scores correlate with the thing you care about — expert holistic ranking, task success, user outcome, tests passing? Spearman ρ between rubric totals and the expert ranking; AUROC of the rubric score for predicting expert accept/reject.
  • Content validity. Does the item set cover the construct? A second expert lists what a bad answer can do wrong and checks each failure has an item (coverage), and that no item measures something irrelevant.
  • Construct validity. Does the rubric measure “clinical safety” or “sounds like a doctor”? Adversarial items — confident wrong answers, hedged right ones — show which. Autorubric’s authors put it plainly: consistency across judges does not establish construct validity.

Discrimination and the rater effects

An item that passes for 98% of candidates (or fails for 98%) is dead weight unless it is a gate; keep it only if it catches a rare, serious failure. Item–total correlation and “which items change the ranking if removed” tell you where the information is. Rater effects to look for in the pilot data: leniency / severity (a rater’s mean shifted), central tendency (3s and 4s on a 1–5 scale), halo (one strong impression colours every item — detect with inter-item correlations far above the expert panel’s), order and anchoring (the first items seen set the scale — randomise), fatigue and drift (agreement with gold falls through the session), and for judges, position, verbosity and self-preference bias.

The κ paradox. With a 95/5 base rate, two raters can agree 94% of the time and score κ ≈ 0.3. Report prevalence with every κ or α, and prefer α with a CI; for judge validation prefer macro-F1, which is why HealthBench uses it.

The pilot protocol

  1. Assemble 30–50 items stratified by quality: a third expected to fail, a third borderline, a third good. Without bad items the agreement statistic is undefined or inflated.
  2. Three raters, blind and independent, after a 30-minute norming session on five anchor examples that are not in the pilot set.
  3. Run the judge on the same items if a model will score in production; its verdicts are a fourth “rater” to be validated, not a replacement for the three.
  4. Compute per criterion: pass rate, α (weighted κ for ordinal), judge–human macro-F1, and for the total: ICC and Spearman ρ against the expert ranking — with bootstrap CIs.
  5. Triage each item: α ≥ 0.80 keep; 0.67–0.80 add examples and re-test; < 0.67 rewrite, split or drop; pass rate > 0.95 or < 0.05 drop unless gating; judge MF1 < 0.75 means the item is not judge-safe yet (keep it human-only).
  6. Read the disagreements with the raters: most are ambiguity in the item, not rater error. Write the edge-case rule you discover into the rubric.
  7. Re-pilot only the changed items on fresh responses; then freeze v1.0 with the reliability table attached.
  8. Re-validate on every rubric version change, whenever the population of responses shifts (new model, new domain), and quarterly regardless.

Sample sizes: for an α confidence interval of roughly ±0.1 you need on the order of 50–100 items where the criterion is live (not NOT_APPLICABLE) across three raters. Rare pitfalls need targeted items so they appear at least ten times.

Pitfalls in validation

  • Agreement on the total only. A rubric score with ICC 0.8 can hide three broken items that cancel out; the unit of validation is the criterion.
  • Only good items in the pilot. No variance, no information; κ undefined or near zero while everyone “agrees”.
  • Judge–judge agreement as validity. Two models trained on the same data agree on the same mistakes. Validate against experts; Autorubric and HealthBench both make this point.
  • Agreement after discussion. Raters who talk before scoring are one rater. Independence first, norming afterwards.
  • Anchors that are the test items. Calibrate on examples outside the pilot and gold sets.
  • The κ paradox ignored. “94% agreement” on a 95/5 item means nothing; report prevalence, α and a CI.
  • Decisions on noise. “v1.1 scored 0.81, v1.0 scored 0.78” on 30 items is one disagreement. Bootstrap the interval before claiming improvement.
  • NOT_APPLICABLE mishandled. Counting it as UNMET punishes refusals; counting it as MET rewards them. Choose a policy per item (SKIP, ZERO, PARTIAL, FAIL) and apply it the same way for humans and the judge.

import numpy as np, pandas as pd
import krippendorff                                   # pip install krippendorff
from sklearn.metrics import cohen_kappa_score, f1_score

def alpha(df: pd.DataFrame, level: str) -> float:
    # reliability matrix: raters × items, NaN where a rater did not score an item
    m = df.pivot(index="rater", columns="item_id", values="value").to_numpy(dtype=float)
    return krippendorff.alpha(reliability_data=m, level_of_measurement=level)   # "nominal" | "ordinal" | "interval"

def boot_ci(df, level, n=1000, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed); items = df.item_id.unique(); vals = []
    for _ in range(n):
        sample = rng.choice(items, size=len(items), replace=True)
        d = pd.concat([df[df.item_id == i].assign(item_id=f"{i}#{j}") for j, i in enumerate(sample)])
        vals.append(alpha(d, level))
    return np.nanpercentile(vals, [2.5, 97.5])

def judge_mf1(human: pd.Series, judge: pd.Series) -> float:
    # human majority verdict vs judge verdict on binary criteria; macro-F1 over MET and UNMET (HealthBench)
    return f1_score(human, judge, average="macro")

The quality system around a rubric

A guideline is a rubric with a procedure and examples

The document raters read has five parts: the purpose in two sentences; the decision procedure in order (for pairwise preference: safety first, then correctness, then instruction adherence, then completeness, then style — a hierarchy, not a sum, so that a prettier wrong answer never wins); the criteria with two or three examples each, including the borderline ones; the reason codes raters attach to a verdict; and what to do when unsure (flag, never guess). Time per item is a design parameter: a guideline that takes four minutes per pair at a two-minute pay rate produces two-minute work.

Across the five data products

  • Preference pairs. A hierarchy rubric with reason codes; randomised position; ties allowed; symmetric 1–7 strength of preference; agreement on the preference is the headline metric (α ≥ 0.8 target), agreement on reason codes the diagnostic.
  • Reasoning data.   • Robotics video. A capture rubric per clip (task visible start to end, hands and objects in frame, lighting, no identifiable bystanders — a gate) and an annotation rubric (segment boundaries within ±0.5 s, taxonomy label exact, no missing segments, object masks above an IoU threshold).
  • Coding tasks. SWE-bench Verified’s QA rubric is the reference: ordinal 0–3 severity for underspecification and for test validity, difficulty buckets by time, three annotators per task, worst-of ensembling, discard at severity ≥ 2 — which removed 68% of the original tasks.

A one-page pairwise-preference guideline, and one gold item

Decision procedure (in this order — stop at the first level that separates A and B)

  1. Safety gate. If one response gives dangerous instructions, discloses private data or complies with a harmful request, the other wins. Reason code SAFETY. If both do, mark both and flag.
  2. Correctness. A factual or logical error that would mislead the user loses to a response without one, however the rest reads. Code WRONG; cite the span.
  3. Instruction adherence. Format, length, language, constraints the user stated. Code INSTR.
  4. Completeness. The response answers every part of the question that can be answered. Code INCOMPLETE.
  5. Helpfulness and clarity. Only now: structure, tone, concision. Codes STYLE, VERBOSE.
  6. Tie if no level separates them; a tie is a valid verdict, not a failure.

Strength of preference on a symmetric scale: −3 (A much better) … 0 (tie) … +3 (B much better). Rationale: one sentence naming the level and the span. Time budget: three minutes per pair.

Setting up an annotation project around a rubric

  1. Rubric v1.0 from card 2, piloted per card 3, with the reliability table.
  2. Guideline: purpose, decision procedure, criteria with examples (good, bad, borderline), reason codes, the unsure path, time budget. Keep it to the length a rater will actually read; move the rest to a searchable appendix.
  3. Qualification test and gold set from the adjudicated pilot items: 20 for qualification, 100+ for gold, refreshed monthly so they cannot be memorised; blind-checked by a second expert.
  4. Pilot batch: 200–500 items, 100% overlap ×3, timing captured. Compute α per criterion, gold accuracy per rater, time per item, adjudication rate.
  5. Calibration on the real disagreements; update the guideline’s examples and edge-case rules; bump the rubric version if any criterion changed.
  6. Production with 10–20% overlap, 5–10% goldens, 1–2% honeypots; automatic pause for raters under 90% gold accuracy; weekly calibration.
  7. Acceptance report per batch: α per criterion and overall, gold accuracy distribution, adjudication rate, rubric pass rates by dimension, defects per 100 on a client-sampled audit, rubric version, rater count and hours.
  8. Drift watch: rolling gold accuracy, α on the last 500 overlapped items, pass-rate shifts after any model or source change; re-pilot on rubric revision.

Pitfalls in annotation projects

  • The essay guideline. Twelve pages of prose, no procedure, no examples of bad; raters invent their own rubric and agreement shows it.
  • Summing where a hierarchy was needed. A beautifully formatted wrong answer out-scores a terse right one. Pairwise preference needs an ordered decision procedure.
  • Gold written by the rubric author alone. The gold inherits the author’s blind spots; a second expert checks gold blind.
  • Gold that leaks. The same 40 gold items for six months; raters recognise them. Refresh, and watch for suspiciously perfect gold accuracy with low overlap agreement.
  • Overlap only in week one. Drift is invisible without continuous overlap; keep 10%.
  • Forced choices. No tie, no not-sure, no not-applicable: raters guess and the guess is noise with a confident label.
  • Speed incentives against the rubric. Paying per item with a four-minute rubric produces one-minute verdicts; measure time and price the rubric honestly (card 10).
  • Rubric edits mid-batch without a version. Half the batch scored under v1.0, half under v1.1, one acceptance report. Version, and re-score a sample for comparability.
  • Disagreement blamed on raters. An adjudication rate above 10% almost always means ambiguous items; fix the rubric first.

# annotation/qc.py — the mechanics around the rubric; platform-agnostic
import random, statistics
from collections import defaultdict

def build_queue(items, gold, honeypots, *, overlap_frac=0.15, gold_frac=0.07, honey_frac=0.015, k=3, seed=7):
    rng = random.Random(seed); queue = []
    for it in items:
        copies = k if rng.random() < overlap_frac else 1          # overlapped items go to k raters
        queue += [{"item": it, "kind": "task"}] * copies
    queue += [{"item": g, "kind": "gold"} for g in rng.sample(gold, int(gold_frac * len(items)))]
    queue += [{"item": h, "kind": "honeypot"} for h in rng.sample(honeypots, int(honey_frac * len(items)))]
    rng.shuffle(queue)                                                 # raters cannot tell which is which
    return queue

def rater_health(verdicts, gold_truth, honey_truth):
    acc, misses = defaultdict(list), defaultdict(int)
    for v in verdicts:
        if v["kind"] == "gold":     acc[v["rater"]].append(v["verdict"] == gold_truth[v["item"]])
        if v["kind"] == "honeypot" and v["verdict"] != honey_truth[v["item"]]: misses[v["rater"]] += 1
    out = {}
    for r, hits in acc.items():
        a = sum(hits) / len(hits)
        out[r] = {"gold_acc": round(a, 3), "honeypot_misses": misses[r],
                  "status": "pause" if a < .90 or misses[r] >= 2 else "warn" if a < .95 or misses[r] == 1 else "ok"}
    return out

def adjudication_queue(verdicts):
    by_item = defaultdict(list)
    for v in verdicts:
        if v["kind"] == "task": by_item[v["item"]].append(v["verdict"])
    return [i for i, vs in by_item.items() if len(vs) > 1 and len(set(vs)) > 1]   # overlapped and not unanimous

def batch_report(rubric_version, alpha_by_criterion, health, adjudicated, n_items, pass_rates, audit_defects_per_100):
    return {
        "rubric_version": rubric_version,
        "alpha": alpha_by_criterion,                                  # per criterion, from card 3's code
        "alpha_overall": round(statistics.mean(alpha_by_criterion.values()), 3),
        "gold_accuracy_median": round(statistics.median(h["gold_acc"] for h in health.values()), 3),
        "adjudication_rate": round(len(adjudicated) / n_items, 3),
        "pass_rates_by_dimension": pass_rates,
        "audit_defects_per_100": audit_defects_per_100,
        "accepted": all(a >= .8 for a in alpha_by_criterion.values()) and len(adjudicated) / n_items < .10,
    }

Building and validating a judge

  1. Make the rubric self-contained (card 2): every fact the judge needs is in the item or in a context field.
  2. Write the template: role; one criterion; the response wrapped as data; rules (independent verdicts, verbatim evidence, no own answer, no length reward, CANNOT_ASSESS allowed); JSON output with a schema.
  3. Decide the call shape: per item for gates and for rubrics over twelve items; batched for cheap optional items once validated.
  4. Set determinism: temperature 0, three to five samples with majority on gates, pinned model version, logged prompt hash.
  5. Pairwise? Swap. Run both orders; inconsistent pairs become ties and are counted.
  6. Validate on the pilot (card 3): per item macro-F1 against the human majority, FAIL recall on gates and essentials, swap consistency, length correlation on human-tied pairs. Thresholds: MF1 ≥ 0.8, FAIL recall ≥ 0.9, swap consistency ≥ 0.9.
  7. Keep humans in the loop: 5–10% overlap in production, all CANNOT_ASSESS and low-confidence verdicts routed to humans, weekly agreement report.
  8. Re-validate on any judge model change, any rubric version change, and when the distribution of responses shifts.

Pitfalls with model judges

  • “Rate this 1–10.” No rubric, no evidence, no diagnosis, position and length biases at full strength.
  • Rubric in the prompt, no evidence out. Verdicts cannot be reviewed and injection cannot be detected.
  • Unmeasured position bias. Pairwise results without a swap are half noise.
  • The judge drafts first. “Write the ideal answer, then compare” grades similarity to the judge’s own mistakes.
  • Same family as the policy. Self-preference inflates scores exactly where you are training.
  • Confidence treated as calibrated. A judge’s 0.9 is not 90%; calibrate (ECE) before routing on it.
  • Unpinned model versions. The judge silently changes and the trend line breaks; pin, and re-validate on upgrade.
  • Twenty items in one call. Items in the middle get skimmed; split.
  • Reference answer as truth. The judge penalises a correct alternative; use the reference as context, the criteria as the test.
  • Judge–judge agreement as validation. Card 3 again: the centre of the target is human.

def judge_item(item: dict, response: str, context: str = "", votes: int = 3) -> ItemVerdict:
    user = (f"<context>{context}</context>\n<criterion id='{item['id']}'>{item['text']}</criterion>\n"
            f"<response>{response}</response>\nThink briefly, then answer as JSON.")
    results = []
    for _ in range(votes):
        r = client.models.generate_content(model=JUDGE_MODEL, contents=user,
            config=types.GenerateContentConfig(system_instruction=SYSTEM, temperature=0.0,
                                               response_mime_type="application/json", response_schema=ItemVerdict))
        v = ItemVerdict.model_validate_json(r.text)
        if v.verdict == "MET" and v.evidence.strip() not in response:     # evidence check: no quote, no MET
            v = ItemVerdict(verdict="UNMET", evidence="", confidence=v.confidence)
        results.append(v)
    winner = Counter(v.verdict for v in results).most_common(1)[0][0]      # majority over votes
    return next(v for v in results if v.verdict == winner)

    return {i["id"]: judge_item(i, response, context, votes=5 if i.get("gate") else 1) for i in rubric["items"]}

def pairwise(prompt: str, a: str, b: str) -> str:
    first  = prefer(prompt, a, b)          # returns "A" | "B" | "TIE" from a pairwise rubric prompt
    second = prefer(prompt, b, a)          # swapped
    swapped = {"A": "B", "B": "A", "TIE": "TIE"}[second]
    return first if first == swapped else "TIE"     # inconsistent → tie, and count it (swap consistency rate)

Explicit or implicit aggregation

Explicit: the judge returns a verdict per item and code computes Σ wi·vi. Legible, auditable, and hackable item by item — a policy can learn to tick boxes. Implicit: the judge sees the whole rubric and the response and returns one score, using the rubric as a lens. Less legible, but it captures interactions (a correct dose stated in a way that would confuse a patient) and RaR found it trained better. The practical hybrid: gates and pitfalls explicit (they must bite deterministically), the quality score implicit, and both logged.

Why this is a data product. Rubric-graded prompt sets are what labs buy as reinforcement-learning environments for non-verifiable domains. The training value is the rubric quality — expert-grounded, self-contained, with pitfalls — which is exactly what cards 2 and 3 produce and measure. The QA report is the product spec.

Building a rubric reward that survives training

  1. Prompts with expert references, stratified by difficulty; include verifiable prompts (exact answers, tests) in the mix so RLVR anchors the run.
  2. A rubric per prompt (card 2): expert-grounded, self-contained, tiers, gates, and at least two pitfalls derived from what weaker candidates actually did.
  3. A validated judge (cards 3 and 5): different family from the policy where possible; evidence-grounded; pinned.
  4. The reward function: gates and pitfalls explicit and deterministic; quality implicit; verifier scores averaged in where a verifier exists (RLCF); smooth enough for the optimiser.
  5. Hold out: a rubric phrasing variant and a human-scored evaluation set the policy never sees; a canary set of grader-directed responses that must keep scoring zero.
  6. Monitors each epoch: mean response length, keyword density of rubric terms, judge-vs-human score gap on the held-out set, verifier agreement, win rate against the previous checkpoint under human pairwise.
  7. Stop or refresh when the held-out human score flattens while the rubric reward keeps rising — that gap is the hack; refresh pitfalls from the new failure modes and continue.

How rubric rewards fail

  • Positive-only checklists. The policy learns to mention every item; coverage rises, usefulness falls. Pitfalls with real negative weight are mandatory.
  • Rubric text visible to the policy. Items leak into prompts or system messages; the model recites them. Keep rubrics on the judge side only.
  • Judge and policy from one family. Self-preference becomes a reward; the run optimises the judge’s taste.
  • Explicit sums over long rubrics. Item-by-item optimisation; interactions ignored; RaR’s result says implicit scoring is the safer default for quality.
  • Hedging rewarded. “Mentions X” items are satisfied by “X may or may not apply”; add a commitment item and a hedging pitfall.
  • No verifiable anchor. A run on rubric rewards alone drifts stylistically; mix verifiable tasks and use verifier scores where they exist.
  • No held-out human eval. The reward curve goes up and nobody notices the model got worse until a customer does. OpenAI’s own guidance: watch for high grader scores with low expert scores.
  • Cost ignored. RLCF’s 25 judge samples per item per response is expensive; 5 samples kept most of the benefit at about half the time. Budget the judge like compute.

# reward.py — gates and pitfalls explicit, quality implicit; usable as a TRL reward function
def rubric_reward(prompt: str, response: str, rubric: dict) -> float:
    verdicts = judge_rubric(rubric, response)                           # card 5: per-item, evidence-checked
    for i in rubric["items"]:
        if i.get("gate") and verdicts[i["id"]].verdict != "MET":
            return 0.0                                                  # a failed gate is a zero, deterministically
    penalty = sum(-i["weight"] for i in rubric["items"]
                  if i["weight"] < 0 and verdicts[i["id"]].verdict == "MET")   # pitfalls that fired
    quality = judge_holistic(prompt, response, rubric)                  # implicit: 0..1 from the judge reading the whole rubric
    verifier = run_verifier(prompt, response)                           # 0/1 where a program exists, else None (RLCF)
    score = quality if verifier is None else 0.5 * (quality + verifier)
    max_pos = sum(i["weight"] for i in rubric["items"] if i["weight"] > 0)
    return max(0.0, score - penalty / max_pos)

Writing an agent rubric

  1. Define success programmatically. The end state, the artefact, the tests. This is the outcome gate; if you cannot write it, the task is not ready for an agent.
  2. Enumerate valid paths. Several orders of calls may be fine; write process criteria as statements about order and arguments, not an exact reference trajectory. Use exact trajectory match only for scenarios with one correct path.
  3. Turn the policy document into gates. One criterion per rule that matters, each paired with a scenario where the simulated user pushes against it.
  4. Add safety gates: forbidden tools and recipients, honeypot calls, data that must not appear in the final response.
  5. Cap efficiency where cost matters; otherwise record it and watch the trend.
  6. Write the final-response rubric as on card 1, plus a grounding check of claims against the trace.
  7. Give the judge a structured trace: numbered calls with arguments and results, the final text, the state diff. Evidence is a call number.
  8. Run each scenario n ≥ 8 times; a run passes when all gates pass and the weighted score clears the threshold; report pass^1, pass^2, pass^4 per scenario and the failing runs.
  9. Encode it in the harness (ADK EvalConfig thresholds: 1.0 on tool-use gates, 0.8 on response quality) and gate CI on it.

Pitfalls in agent evaluation

  • Exact trajectory match. Every valid alternative path fails; teams then loosen it to nothing. Use in-order or any-order matching, or statements.
  • Final answer only. “Paid 184.00 on 19 Oct” can be true of a run that paid before approval. Score the trace.
  • Raw logs to the judge. Ten thousand tokens of spans; the judge misses the one call that matters. Summarise structurally.
  • One run. Agents are stochastic; one pass is an anecdote. pass^k or nothing.
  • Efficiency as pass/fail by default. A slower correct run is not a failure; ADK keeps the counters informational for this reason. Cap only where cost is the requirement.
  • Policy rubric without pressure. Rules nobody tests against an insistent user pass trivially; τ-bench’s simulated user is the point.
  • The judge cannot see the world. Without the state diff it grades the agent’s description of what it did.
  • Per-step rubrics only. Each step locally reasonable, the episode globally wrong (three correct lookups, no payment). Keep an episode-level outcome gate.
  • Unmeasured simulated user. If the user simulator drifts from the persona, the agent is evaluated on the wrong task; ADK’s per_turn_user_simulator_quality_v1 exists for this.

From verdicts to a score

S = clip( Σᵢ wᵢ·vᵢ / Σ_{wᵢ>0} wᵢ , 0, 1 ) vᵢ ∈ {0,1} for binary, (level−1)/(L−1) for ordinal S_final = (Π_gates 1[vg = MET]) · S

Normalising by the positive maximum (HealthBench, Autorubric) keeps penalties real: a −10 pitfall on a 29-point rubric costs a third of the score. Normalising by the total of absolute weights makes pitfalls shrink the denominator and quietly stops them biting. Gates multiply (or go first, lexicographically): a failed gate is a zero whatever else was earned, and a gate is not the same as a −10 — twenty-four points of positives can outvote a −10, which is the argument for making must-pass items gates rather than heavy penalties.

Tiers, trees and implicit scores

Tiers to weights. Essential 10, Important 5, Optional 2 is a common mapping; XpertBench carries both a tier and a 1–10 weight per checkpoint. Trees. PaperBench’s rubrics are hierarchical: a paper’s replication decomposes into sections, then into 8,316 leaf tasks of three types (code development, execution, result match); leaves are judged binary and each node’s score is the weighted mean of its children, up to a 0–100 root. Weights express the authors’ view of importance; the tree keeps a long rubric legible. Implicit. When the judge returns one holistic number after reading the rubric (RaR), combine it explicitly with the deterministic parts: gates × (α·implicit + (1−α)·explicit), and write α down.

Abstention, judges, and datasets

Over a dataset: the mean of per-example scores (HealthBench: per-example scores may go negative; the overall mean is clipped to [0,1]), reported by dimension, by difficulty bucket and as a pass rate at the accept threshold.

Writing the decision rule

  1. Name the decision the score serves (accept an item, rank systems, reward a policy, pay a vendor) — the rule differs for each.
  2. Gates first: list the must-pass items; they multiply, they are not weights.
  3. Weights from tiers; at least two pitfalls with negative weights; normalise by the positive maximum.
  4. Ordinal items mapped to 0–1 by level; never averaged as raw levels.
  5. Abstention policy per item; the same for humans and judge.
  6. Implicit component if used: fix α and log both numbers.
  7. Threshold from the pilot’s expert accept/reject labels via ROC; record the precision and recall of the gate at that threshold.
  8. Intervals on every reported mean and pass rate; paired tests for every comparison; by dimension and difficulty.
  9. Write it into the rubric file (decision: in card 1’s YAML) and test the scoring code with hand-computed examples.

Pitfalls in scoring

  • Equal weights. The rubric becomes a word count of good things; the longest answer wins.
  • Normalising by total absolute weight. Adding a pitfall lowers the denominator and the pitfall stops mattering.
  • Gates as −10s. A must-pass item that can be outvoted is not a must-pass item.
  • Averaging ordinal levels. “3.4 out of 5” assumes level 4 is as far from 3 as 2 is from 1; map to 0–1 with anchors, or report the distribution.
  • No interval. 0.71 vs 0.68 becomes a decision; with n = 200 it is noise.
  • Different items for different systems. Unpaired comparisons mix system difference with item difficulty.
  • Threshold by eye. “0.8 feels right” has no precision or recall attached; derive it from expert labels.
  • Clipping per item. HealthBench clips the overall mean, not the example; clipping each example at 0 hides how bad the worst answers were. Choose, and say which.
  • Averaging humans with judges. Humans are the reference; the judge is the estimator. Report judge agreement with humans, not a blended score.
  • Simpson’s paradox across difficulty. A better on easy and on hard, worse overall because B got more easy items; stratify and pair.

# scoring.py
import numpy as np
from sklearn.metrics import roc_curve, precision_score, recall_score

def tree_score(node, verdicts) -> float:
    """node: {'id','weight','children':[...]} or a leaf {'id','weight'}; leaves are binary verdicts."""
    if "children" not in node:
        return float(verdicts[node["id"]])
    kids = node["children"]
    return sum(k["weight"] * tree_score(k, verdicts) for k in kids) / sum(k["weight"] for k in kids)

def flat_score(items, verdicts, abstain="SKIP") -> float:
    """items: [{'id','weight','gate','type','levels'}]; verdicts: id → 0/1, level, or None (cannot assess)."""
    for i in items:
        if i.get("gate") and verdicts.get(i["id"]) != 1: return 0.0
    total, max_pos = 0.0, 0.0
    for i in items:
        if i.get("gate"): continue
        v, w = verdicts.get(i["id"]), i["weight"]
        if v is None:
            if abstain == "SKIP": continue                    # out of the denominator too
            v = {"ZERO": 0.0, "PARTIAL": 0.5, "FAIL": None}[abstain]
            if v is None: return 0.0
        elif i["type"] == "ordinal":
            v = (v - 1) / (i["levels"] - 1)
        if w > 0: max_pos += w
        total += w * v
    return float(np.clip(total / max_pos, 0, 1)) if max_pos else 0.0

def boot_mean_ci(scores, n=2000, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed); s = np.asarray(scores)
    means = [s[rng.integers(0, len(s), len(s))].mean() for _ in range(n)]
    return s.mean(), np.percentile(means, [2.5, 97.5])

def paired_boot(a, b, n=2000, seed=0):
    """a, b: scores of two systems on the SAME items, same order."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed); d = np.asarray(a) - np.asarray(b)
    diffs = [d[rng.integers(0, len(d), len(d))].mean() for _ in range(n)]
    lo, hi = np.percentile(diffs, [2.5, 97.5])
    return d.mean(), (lo, hi), not (lo <= 0 <= hi)        # mean difference, CI, "distinguishable"

def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):
    p = k / n; den = 1 + z*z/n; centre = (p + z*z/(2*n)) / den
    half = z * np.sqrt(p*(1-p)/n + z*z/(4*n*n)) / den
    return centre - half, centre + half

def choose_threshold(scores, expert_accept):
    """pick accept_at from expert accept/reject labels on the pilot: Youden's J, then report the gate's precision/recall."""
    fpr, tpr, thr = roc_curve(expert_accept, scores)
    t = thr[np.argmax(tpr - fpr)]
    pred = np.asarray(scores) >= t
    return t, precision_score(expert_accept, pred), recall_score(expert_accept, pred)

Pitfalls on the platform

  • Fresh rubrics per run. Candidate A was tested against one generated set, candidate B against another; the comparison is not paired. Freeze a group.

  • Metric versions unpinned. A managed metric revision moves the trend line; pin and re-baseline.
  • Explanation mistaken for evidence. Managed metrics return an explanation; for your custom rubrics require quotes.
  • Expecting policy knowledge. No managed metric knows your refund policy; that is a custom metric or ADK rubrics.
  • Cost surprise. Rubric generation plus per-rubric scoring is many calls per example; sample during iteration, run the full set at the gate.

  4. Kickoff with the client. A rubric review workshop: walk ten real items together, score them, argue, edit; sign the version; calibrate the client’s own auditors on the same anchors so their audit matches the rubric.
  5. Per-batch report (card 4) under the contract’s version; disputes to adjudication; change requests to change control.
  6. Pricing from the pilot. Measured time per item at the measured overlap and gold fraction; criteria added later are priced as a change.
  7. Retire rubrics whose datasets are done; archive with their statistics so a future project can reuse the global layer.

Operational anti-patterns

  • No owner. A rubric by committee drifts with whoever edits the guideline last.
  • Edits without versions. Half a batch under one meaning, half under another, one report.
  • Comparisons across major versions. “Quality went up 6 points” after criteria were removed.
  • The judge upgraded silently. The platform or the team changes the model; agreement moves; nobody attributes it.
  • “We’ll write the rubric after the pilot.” The pilot then measures nothing; rubric first, pilot second.
  • SLAs in adjectives. “High quality, expert-level” in a contract guarantees a dispute; pass rates by dimension under a version do not.
  • Client audits by taste. Auditors who were never calibrated reject items the rubric accepts; calibrate them at kickoff.
  • Rubric complexity unpriced. Twenty-five criteria with evidence at a ten-criterion price produces ten-criterion work.
  • Gold that never changes. Memorised within a month; refresh from adjudicated items.

“How do you think about rubrics?”

Thirty secondsA rubric is the contract that turns “good” into checkable statements: observable, one thing each, binary where possible, anchored where degree matters, weighted by consequence, with gates and pitfalls, and a written decision rule. I derive it from a reference answer and candidate responses, validate it per criterion against human raters, and then the same object runs as the annotation guideline, the judge prompt, the training reward, the agent eval and the acceptance criteria in the contract.

The long form, in the order to say it.

What it is. A rubric decomposes a judgement into criteria with scales, weights and a rule for combining them. It sits in the middle of the verifiability spectrum, between tests and taste, and the discipline is to push every check as far toward tests as it will go: a program where a program exists, a binary with the fact stated where a fact can be stated, an anchored scale only where degree matters, a holistic score only as a tie-break.

How it is written. Not from the spec alone. From the intent, an expert reference answer, and a few candidate responses from weak to strong: the criteria are whatever separates the candidates. A model drafts, an expert edits, a second expert reviews. Each item is observable, atomic, self-contained — the fact is inside the statement so a non-expert or a small model can check it — tiered, with gates for must-pass and pitfalls for the failures that look fine superficially, and length-neutral.

How it is validated. As an instrument, per criterion: a pilot of thirty to fifty items that include bad ones, three independent raters and the judge as a fourth; pass rate for discrimination, Krippendorff’s α with a bootstrap interval for reliability, macro-F1 against the human majority for whether the judge can run it, Spearman against an expert ranking for whether the total means anything. Agreement is not validity — the centre of the target is always human.

Where it runs. As the annotation guideline, written as a decision procedure with examples, inside a quality system of qualification, gold items, overlap, adjudication and calibration. As the judge prompt, one criterion per call, evidence quoted verbatim so a human can check it in five seconds and text addressed to the evaluator is ignored, position swapped for pairwise, pinned model version. As the training reward — implicit for quality, explicit for gates and pitfalls, mixed with verifiable tasks and watched by a human-scored held-out set because the policy will learn the rubric if you let it. As the agent eval, attached to the trajectory: outcome by program, process as statements, policy as gates under user pressure, efficiency as counters, a final-response rubric, and pass^k over eight runs.

How it is scored and run. Gates multiply; a weighted sum normalised by the positive maximum; abstention policies; thresholds from an ROC against expert labels; intervals on every mean and paired comparisons. Versioned like code with a registry, re-baselined on major changes, monitored for drift in raters and judges. On Google Cloud, the Gen AI evaluation service’s adaptive rubrics generated once and frozen as a group, custom metrics for domain criteria, ADK’s rubric-based criteria for agents — and still a human overlap once to validate the managed judge on my domain. With a client, the rubric is the acceptance criteria and the thing that makes the price explainable.

Questions they ask, and the shape of the answer

  • “What makes a good rubric?”Observable, atomic, self-contained items; binary where possible, anchored where not; tiers, gates and pitfalls; length-neutral; derived from candidates, not the spec; validated per criterion; versioned. (Cards 1–3)
  • “How many criteria?”Eight to twenty. HealthBench averages about ten per conversation, XpertBench seventeen; fewer than six is holistic in disguise, more than twenty has not been pruned by a pilot. (Card 2)
  • “How do you know raters agree because the rubric is good and not because the task is easy?”Pilot items include bad ones; per-criterion pass rates show discrimination; α with a CI, prevalence reported; and validity against an expert ranking. (Card 3)
  • “Can an LLM write the rubric?”It can draft one well from a reference and candidates; an expert edits and a second reviews. Toloka and XpertBench both do it that way. At scoring time the model executes the rubric; it does not author it. (Cards 2, 5)
  • “Why not just a 1–10 judge score?”Low agreement, no diagnosis, full exposure to position and length bias, and nothing a client can audit. Rubrics give per-item verdicts with evidence. (Cards 1, 5)
  • “Explicit or implicit scoring?”Gates and pitfalls explicit; quality implicit if a validated judge is available — RaR found implicit trained better — and both logged. (Cards 6, 8)
  • “How do you stop reward hacking?”Pitfalls for what the policy will try, rubric never visible to the policy, a different judge family, verifiers mixed in, a human-scored held-out set that stops the run when the reward diverges. (Card 6)
  • “How do you evaluate an agent with rubrics?”Outcome by program, process as statements on the trace, policy gates under user pressure, efficiency counters, final-response rubric; eight runs and pass^k. ADK has these criteria built in. (Card 7)
  • “What is an adaptive rubric?”Google’s per-prompt generated pass/fail checks with explanations; generate once, review, freeze as a group for fair comparison; validate the managed judge on your domain once. (Card 9)
  • “How do you price annotation?”From the pilot’s measured time per item at the chosen overlap and gold fraction; criteria count and the evidence requirement drive it; figures in a proposal are illustrative until the pilot. (Card 10)
  • “What goes in the contract?”Rubric id and version, pass rates by dimension, α on live criteria, gold accuracy, adjudication rate, audit defect rate, dispute resolution by adjudication, change control with re-baselining. (Card 10)

Before a rubric is piloted

  1. The intent and the decision the score drives are written in one sentence.
  2. The rubric was derived from a reference answer and at least three candidates of different quality.
  3. Every item describes what the response does or contains — no “understands”, “demonstrates”, “shows expertise”.
  4. No item contains two checks (“accurate and complete”).
  5. No undefined qualifier (“appropriate”, “adequate”, “sufficient”, “good”).
  6. Every fact the judge needs is inside the item or in a supplied context field.
  7. Binary wherever possible; every ordinal level has a behavioural anchor and an example.
  8. Nothing is satisfied by writing more; there are pitfalls for padding, hedging and criteria recitation.
  9. At least one gate exists for the must-pass conditions, and gates are gates, not large negative weights.
  10. Tiers or weights are assigned, and normalisation is by the positive maximum.
  11. Programmatic checks (length, format, forbidden strings, tests) are verifiers, not judge items.
  12. Rules exist for refusals, partial answers, off-topic responses and CANNOT_ASSESS.
  13. Items a human can check in under ten seconds each; the rubric fits the time budget.
  14. Eight to twenty items, or a tree with reportable nodes.
  15. A second expert reviewed the items and the coverage against the failure-mode list.
  16. The decision rule (score, threshold, gates, abstention) is written in the rubric file.
  17. The pilot plan exists: 30–50 stratified items, three raters, the judge as a fourth, statistics per criterion.
  18. The rubric has an id, a version, an owner and a provenance block.
  19. Anchor examples for calibration are not in the pilot or gold sets.
  20. If used as a training reward: the rubric text is invisible to the policy, and a human-scored held-out set exists.
