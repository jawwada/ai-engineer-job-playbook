1 · FinOps as a discipline: the 2026 Framework, the people, the cadence

FinOps is the operating practice that makes engineering, finance and product decide together what technology spending is worth. The FinOps Foundation’s 2026 Framework (March 2026) defines it as maximising the business value of technology — not cloud alone — through three phases, four domains and twenty-two capabilities, run by named personas on a monthly cadence and measured on a Crawl, Walk, Run scale.

Standing up a practice: the first 90 days

  1. Days 1–30: see the money. Enable the billing exports (standard, detailed, pricing — and the FOCUS export), tie one month to the invoices, map accounts and projects to the organisation, measure tag compliance by cost, publish the first showback with a “unallocated” line nobody can hide.
  2. Days 31–60: make it a number people act on. Shared-cost pools with written methods; a forecast with a known-changes register and an accuracy log; anomaly routing with severities; the first commitment position (coverage, utilisation, expiries).
  3. Days 61–90: make it a practice. The monthly cadence above; a savings pipeline with scoring and owners; unit economics for the two products that matter; the executive one-pager; a single-source-of-truth agreement with whoever else publishes numbers.
  4. Then: assess. Score each capability Crawl/Walk/Run against the markers, pick three to move this year, and put AI spend in scope from the start — it is the fastest-growing and least allocated line in most estates.

Where practices stall

  • Inform forever. Dashboards everyone admires and nobody acts on; no owner per number, no decision per report.
  • FinOps as a tax. A central team that reports engineers’ waste upward instead of handing them a sized, reproducible opportunity in their own units.
  • Two sets of numbers. Finance’s view and engineering’s view differ by definition (billed vs amortised, with or without credits) and the meeting is spent reconciling instead of deciding.
  • Capabilities without maturity targets. “We do forecasting” with no accuracy log is Crawl claiming Walk.
  • AI spend outside the practice. Tokens billed to a shared project, attributed to nobody, growing 6% a month.
  • Rules changed mid-year. Allocation methods and tagging standards edited during the year destroy comparability; change them at fiscal boundaries.

The sentences

If asked “what is FinOps?”An operating practice, not a tool: engineering, finance and product deciding together what technology spend is worth. The 2026 Framework describes it as three phases in a loop — inform, optimise, operate — across four domains and twenty-two capabilities, each with a Crawl-Walk-Run maturity. In practice it is a monthly cadence: tie the month to the invoices, allocate it fully, explain the variance, reforecast with an accuracy log, run a savings pipeline with owners, and put three numbers and a decision in front of leadership.
The line that shows you follow the practice as it is nowThe 2026 release renamed Workload Optimization to Usage Optimization, folded cloud into “technology spend” so SaaS, licences and AI are in scope, and added Executive Strategy Alignment — which is the capability that says a report exists to support a decision. Ninety-eight percent of practices now manage some AI spend, so token economics is FinOps, not a side topic.

2 · Billing data & cost metrics: the numbers two analysts can agree on

Every cloud reports several “costs” for the same usage, differing in whether commitment fees appear when paid or spread over the usage they cover, whether negotiated discounts are included, and whether credits are netted. Using the wrong one is the most common reason two people disagree about the same month. The fix is a source of record (the billing export in BigQuery, increasingly in the FOCUS shape) and a table that says which metric answers which question.

From list price to what a team is shown

Google Cloud: hierarchy, exports, tools

A Cloud Billing account (self-serve or invoiced) pays for projects; the resource hierarchy is organisation → folders → projects. Two allocation mechanisms coexist: labels (not inherited, carry cost only from when applied, appear as labels, project.labels, system_labels) and Resource Manager tags (IAM-governed, inherited, in the tags field, supported on some resource types). The BigQuery export is the source of record: standard usage cost (hourly rows by SKU and project), detailed usage cost (adds resource.name and is required for GKE cost allocation), pricing, CUD metadata (preview), and FOCUS (preview since June 2026, a Google-managed linked dataset with two-year retention). Reports group by charge period (formerly usage date) or billing period (formerly invoice month). Around it: Billing reports with a Savings filter, the cost table (built to match the invoice), the cost breakdown waterfall (on-demand → negotiated savings → savings programmes → taxes → total), budgets (fixed or last period, thresholds on actual or forecast, Pub/Sub for automation; spend-cap budgets in preview since July 2026 for the Gemini API, Agent Platform and Cloud Run), anomaly detection (GA October 2025), FinOps hub 2.0 with usage insights and a FinOps score, Recommender, and the FinOps Explainability Agent (GA April 2026) that breaks AI cost down by model, API key and token type.

On Google Cloud the same economics: a resource-based CUD charged through its fee SKU as the term runs. The $7,200 fee is its own SKU; covered usage shows $9,500 of cost with −$9,500 of CUD credits; other usage $2,375 of cost ($2,500 at list). Summing cost gives $19,075 and counts both; cost plus credits gives $9,575. Under the new spend-based data model the covered usage appears at the discounted price and the fee row carries FEE_UTILIZATION_OFFSET credits — gross cost changes, cost plus credits does not.

Building the source of record

  1. Enable the exports on day one: standard, detailed and pricing on Google Cloud (multi-region datasets backfill from the start of the previous month; regional ones start at enablement), plus the FOCUS export; CUR 2.0 / Data Exports on AWS; the FOCUS export on Azure.
  2. Land them in one warehouse with a date-partitioned table per cloud and a FOCUS-shaped view on top; keep the raw tables.
  3. Tie out: billed cost by invoice equals the invoice total; log restatements (ChargeClass = Correction) and late data.
  4. Define the metrics in views (effective_cost, billed_cost, list_cost) once; every report reads the views, nobody re-derives them.
  5. Price table for AI calls joined to token usage from traces, reconciled to the provider bill monthly (card 6).
  6. Data-quality checks daily: row counts by day, cost by service vs yesterday, missing labels, negative effective cost outside credits.
  7. Timing rules written down: when data lands (hours; backfills up to five days; pricing up to 48 hours), when a month is final (BD10), and what “final” means for each cloud.

Data pitfalls

  • Google cost without credits. Overstates by the whole CUD programme, and the overstatement changes when the spend-based data model migrates.
  • Dashboards on the credit columns after migration to the new spend-based model: the discount disappears from them because it is now a lower price, not a credit.
  • Blended cost. Hides which account benefited from a commitment; use it for almost nothing.
  • Summing ListCost over purchase and usage rows. Double-counts commitments.
  • Amortised view for the invoice meeting. Finance sees cash; show billed.
  • Trends on billed cost. Every upfront purchase is a spike that is not usage.
  • Labels applied late. They carry cost only from the time they were applied; history stays unallocated.
  • Time zones. Google’s console days start at midnight US Pacific; your warehouse day may not.

3 · Allocation & unit economics: from the bill to the cost of one unit of value

Allocation turns a bill into a cost per application, team and product; unit economics turns that into a cost per transaction, conversation or resolved ticket — the only cost number a product owner can act on. It starts with the hierarchy, needs a tagging standard that is enforced and measured by cost, a written method for every shared pool, and models for the two things that resist allocation: Kubernetes clusters and AI tokens.

The allocation waterfall

A tagging standard that works on three clouds

Four required keys — application, environment, cost-center, owner — in lowercase kebab-case because Google labels accept only lowercase; values from the service catalogue, the chart of accounts and the team directory, with cost centre and owner derived from application so one key carries the other two. Enforcement in infrastructure-as-code on each cloud (policy that blocks creation without the keys; Google Resource Manager tags where inheritance helps), and compliance measured strictly, by cost: one invalid value on a big account is worth more than a hundred untagged sandboxes.

Kubernetes and AI

The OpenCost model (behind OpenCost, Kubecost and the AKS add-on) prices a workload at the larger of what it requested and what it used — the scheduler reserved the request — and defines idle as cluster cost minus workload costs. Policy decides who pays idle: all to the platform team (tenants never right-size requests), all proportional (the platform team never bin-packs), or the hybrid — proportional up to a target idle percentage, the excess to the platform team. GKE cost allocation needs the detailed export and adds k8s-namespace, k8s-workload-name, kube:system-overhead and kube:unallocated labels; AWS split cost allocation data and the AKS add-on do the same. For AI spend, three sources combine: gateway virtual keys, the provider’s own allocation (Bedrock inference profiles with tags; Google projects, labels and API keys), and OpenTelemetry token attributes joined to a versioned price table — reconciled to the provider bill monthly, because a gap above a few percent means traffic bypassing the gateway or a wrong price.

Unit economics

Fully loaded cost ÷ the unit the business counts: cost per transaction for payments, per conversation or per resolved ticket for an assistant, per document for extraction, per GB-month for a data platform. Trended monthly, with volume beside it, it separates “we grew” from “we got less efficient”, and it is the number a product owner can defend in a pricing conversation. For AI products the unit cost decomposes further: tokens per unit × price per token, by model and route (card 6).

Getting allocation above 90%

  1. Map the hierarchy first. Projects and folders (accounts, subscriptions) to applications and cost centres; most spend allocates here before any tag exists.
  2. Publish the tagging standard with the four keys, value catalogues and derivation rules; put it in the IaC modules so the default is compliant.
  3. Measure compliance by cost, rank violations by dollars, fix the top ones at the source (the IaC default), then block at creation.
  4. Name three to five shared pools, write a method for each, show shared cost on its own line, and change rules only at fiscal-year boundaries.
  5. Turn on platform allocation: GKE cost allocation (detailed export), split cost allocation on EKS, the AKS add-on, or OpenCost across clouds; publish idle monthly with a target.
  6. Allocate AI spend by project or label per team, API keys per feature, gateway keys per tenant; join to a price table; reconcile monthly.
  7. Define the units with product owners (what they count, from which system), compute fully loaded unit cost monthly, trend it with volume.
  8. Showback for three months with the exact chargeback file before any chargeback; chargeback needs a file that ties to the bill, a suspense cost centre, budgets transferred at a fiscal boundary and a dispute process.

Allocation pitfalls

  • Tags before hierarchy. A year of tagging campaigns when a project-to-application map would have allocated 80% on day one.
  • Compliance by count. 95% of resources tagged, 60% of cost; measure by dollars.
  • Methods chosen after seeing the numbers. Every team lobbies for the method that favours it; agree the method, then compute.
  • Too many pools. Twelve pools with twelve rules nobody can explain; keep three to five.
  • Idle to the platform team by default. Tenants over-request forever; use the hybrid with a target.
  • Commitment benefits moving cost silently. When one account’s usage jumps it takes part of the shared discount and every other team’s rate rises with no change in their usage; decide a policy (benefit follows usage, group sharing, or blended-rate showback with savings and waste on a central line).
  • AI spend in one shared project. Unallocatable by construction; keys and labels per team from day one.
  • Unit metrics the business does not count. “Cost per API call” when the product sells seats; use their unit.

-- label compliance measured by cost, with the top violations ranked (Google standard export)
WITH rows AS (
  SELECT project.id,
         (SELECT value FROM UNNEST(labels) WHERE key = 'application') AS application,
         (SELECT value FROM UNNEST(labels) WHERE key = 'environment') AS environment,
         cost + IFNULL((SELECT SUM(c.amount) FROM UNNEST(credits) c), 0) AS net_cost
  FROM `billing.gcp_billing_export_v1_XXXXXX`
  WHERE invoice.month = '202609' AND cost_type = 'regular')
SELECT COALESCE(application, '(missing)') AS application,
       SUM(net_cost) AS net_cost,
       SUM(net_cost) / SUM(SUM(net_cost)) OVER () AS share_of_cost,
       application IS NOT NULL AND application IN (SELECT name FROM `catalog.applications`) AS compliant
FROM rows GROUP BY 1, 4 ORDER BY compliant, net_cost DESC;

The math

coverage = covered eligible usage ÷ total eligible usage utilization = used commitment ÷ total commitment waste = 1 − utilization break-even u* = 1 − d optimum: the level usage exceeds in (1 − d) of hours = the (1 − d)-quantile from the bottom
ESR (Foundation, option 1) = (commitment savings − unused commitment) ÷ on-demand-equivalent spend ≈ utilization × coverage × discount ESR (FOCUS) = (ContractedCost − EffectiveCost) ÷ ContractedCost on usage rows

A commitment costing C buys usage worth C ÷ (1 − d) at on-demand rates; at utilisation u it delivers u·C ÷ (1 − d), which equals its cost when u = 1 − d. Deep three-year discounts tolerate a lot of waste; shallow one-year discounts very little. The Foundation suggests an upper waterline near 80% utilisation for resource-based commitments on steady-state usage; spend-based plans follow usage across families and regions, so 95% or more is reasonable for them. Quote a savings-rate benchmark only with its definition and population: a 2025 AWS-compute survey put the median effective savings rate near 15% overall, 0% under $500k of annual compute and 38% above $10M.

The purchase procedure

  1. Hourly eligible usage for 60–90 days, at on-demand-equivalent dollars for spend-based plans, hours for resource-based ones; daily data gives a lower floor than hourly and must be said so.
  2. Remove what is leaving (decommission dates from the known-changes register) and add committed growth with dates.
  3. Compute the curve: for each candidate level, utilisation, coverage, weekly savings and ESR; find the plateau around the (1 − d)-quantile.
  4. Take the plateau’s lower edge: the smallest commitment within a few percent of the best net savings.
  5. Split by term: three years for the floor you are sure of, one year for the next layer, nothing for the top layer or for workloads with an end date.
  6. Ladder: monthly or quarterly tranches, so expiries are spread; alert 60 days before every expiry.
  7. Prefer the spend-based form when a machine family, architecture or region move is likely within the term; do not count on undo options (Google has none).
  8. Write the recommendation with the sizing, the downside table, the consumption-commitment position and Finance’s approval in one document; track utilisation monthly after purchase.

Commitment pitfalls

  • Sizing to the average. Half the hours are below it; the commitment is wasted in every one of them.
  • Buying before checking what leaves. Coverage 56% and utilisation 100% looks like “buy more” until the data platform switches off in November and utilisation falls to 96.5% on the existing plan.
  • One big purchase, one expiry. A cliff; ladder.
  • Comparing hourly commitments across data models. A $100-an-hour commitment means different coverage under the legacy (on-demand dollars) and new (discounted dollars) spend-based models.
  • Modelling sustained-use discounts for an invoiced account. They apply to self-serve accounts and eligible series only.
  • Region-bound commitments and a region move. Resource-based commitments do not follow the workload.
  • Quoting an ESR without its definition. FOCUS, Foundation option 1 and option 2 differ by whether negotiated discounts and unused commitment are in; the same month gives 5.7%, 5.4% and 9.6%.
  • Commitments for AI sized from a growth month. AI spend is young and volatile; commit to its floor, with the gen-AI savings plan’s shallower discount in mind.

# commitments/size.py — the curve, the plateau, the recommendation (spend-based plan; usage in on-demand $/hour)
import numpy as np

def curve(hourly_usage: np.ndarray, discount: float, levels: np.ndarray):
    """hourly_usage: eligible on-demand $/h after removing what leaves; levels: candidate on-demand-equivalent $/h."""
    rows = []
    for L in levels:
        covered = np.minimum(hourly_usage, L)                 # on-demand $ covered each hour
        commit_cost = L * (1 - discount) * len(hourly_usage)   # paid whether used or not
        used = covered.sum() * (1 - discount)                  # value delivered at plan rates
        on_demand_rest = (hourly_usage - covered).sum()
        total = commit_cost + on_demand_rest
        baseline = hourly_usage.sum()
        rows.append({"level": L, "hourly_commit_$": L * (1 - discount),
                     "utilization": used / commit_cost, "coverage": covered.sum() / baseline,
                     "savings": baseline - total, "esr": (baseline - total) / baseline})
    return rows

def recommend(hourly_usage, discount, tolerance=0.05):
    q = np.quantile(hourly_usage, 1 - discount)               # expected-value optimum: the (1−d) quantile from the bottom
    levels = np.linspace(hourly_usage.min(), hourly_usage.max(), 200)
    rows = curve(hourly_usage, discount, levels)
    best = max(r["savings"] for r in rows)
    plateau = [r for r in rows if r["savings"] >= (1 - tolerance) * best]
    return {"break_even_utilization": 1 - discount, "optimum_level": float(q),
            "recommended": min(plateau, key=lambda r: r["level"]),   # the plateau's lower edge
            "downside_10pct": curve(hourly_usage * 0.9, discount, np.array([min(plateau, key=lambda r: r["level"])["level"]]))[0]}

  • Savings claimed from the bill total. Volume moved too; measure against a counterfactual by meter.

6 · FinOps for AI: token economics — what a token costs, why agents are quadratic, and the ten levers

A model bill is token counts times per-token prices, and the prices differ by an order of magnitude by kind of token: cached input is roughly a tenth to a quarter of the input rate, output several times it, and reasoning is billed as output whether or not you see it. An agent that takes ten turns does not cost ten times one turn; it costs closer to fifty, because every turn re-sends everything before it. Knowing the kinds is the whole of AI FinOps literacy; the rest is arithmetic and ten levers in order of leverage.

Why agent loops are quadratic

Models are stateless between calls: each call receives the whole conversation and produces the next step. At turn n the context is a fixed prefix P (system prompt, tool schemas, retrieved documents) plus about g tokens per turn of accumulated output and tool results. Summed over N turns the input is N·P + g·N(N+1)/2: the first term is what people expect, the second is what the bill shows.

Caching: a prefix, byte-identical, first

Prefill computes the keys and values for every token; that KV cache depends only on the tokens read so far, so two requests that begin with the same 12,000 tokens can share the work. Two consequences: the match is on a prefix (a change at token 500 invalidates everything after it) and it is exact (a timestamp in the system prompt, a reordered tool list, a user name before the static instructions — each is a miss). Order the prompt from most to least stable: system instructions, tool definitions, shared reference documents, conversation history, the latest tool result. In a loop the history itself becomes the prefix, so turn n is mostly a hit on what turn n−1 sent; with an 8,000-token prefix and prior history cached at a tenth, the ten-turn run’s input bill falls from 162,500 full-rate tokens to the equivalent of about 30,000 — and output and thinking are untouched. On Gemini, implicit caching is on by default with minimum prompt sizes of 2,048 tokens on 2.5 models and 4,096 on 3.x, hits are reported in the usage object, and explicit CachedContent objects with a TTL bill storage per token-hour; other vendors expose explicit breakpoints with a write premium. Measure the hit ratio (cached ÷ total input) per workload as a first-class metric: a drop after a deploy means something dynamic moved early in the prompt.

The levers, in order of leverage

  1. Prompt caching. Stable prefix first; serialise tools in a fixed order; compact history at checkpoints, not every call. Agents with long tool lists see 50–90% input-cost reductions.
  2. Model routing. Classify difficulty (rules, a small model, confidence) and send the easy 70–90% to the cheap tier; frontier for hard cases and final synthesis; quality measured per route on the eval set.
  3. Context engineering. Rerank to 5–8 chunks; trim tool outputs to what the next step needs; summarise history; drop dead tool schemas; sub-agents with fresh windows instead of one bloated conversation.
  4. Output control. Structured outputs, length limits, stop sequences; thinking budgets per step and none for workers; lower the thinking level rather than capping output tokens, because a cap that cuts off thinking still bills it.
  5. Batch APIs for anything offline — evals, backfills, nightly classification, embeddings — at about half price.
  6. Response and embedding caches. Exact and semantic caches for repeated questions; never re-embed unchanged chunks.
  7. Right-size the architecture. Fewer agent hops; deterministic code for deterministic steps; a classifier where a classifier will do; distil high-volume tasks to small models.
  8. Self-host where volume justifies it. Open-weight models on vLLM at steady high utilisation beat per-token prices above a threshold — include the ops cost.
  9. Provisioned capacity only when utilisation is proven. Capacity costs while idle; on-demand with fallbacks until traffic is steady, then commit for the base load (Google: provisioned throughput, and the gen-AI Flexible Savings Plan for the spend floor).
  10. Stop waste. Retry storms, loops, duplicate calls from parallel workers, logging full prompts twice, evaluation jobs on frontier tiers when a cheap judge suffices.

Finding what consumes the most, then cutting it

  1. Record cost where it happens. Every model call is a span with model, the four token counts (input, cached, output, thinking), workload, agent, step name, the tool that preceded it, the prompt version; every tool observation records its output’s token count. OpenTelemetry’s gen_ai.usage.* attributes and the gen_ai.client.token.usage metric carry this; Langfuse computes cost from a model price table.
  2. Three views. Bill by workload and model over time, split by token type; cost by step name within a workload; the most expensive traces of the day, sorted.
  3. Read the top ten traces. Input growing by a large constant each step means a big tool result in the history; thinking dominating means the effort level is too high for the step; many small generations means a loop.
  4. Compute the carry cost per tool: tokens a tool introduced into the history × the number of turns that followed. A 5,000-token result at step 2 of a 12-step run costs more than a 20,000-token result at step 11.
  5. Check the cache hit ratio per generation. Zero after the first turn means the prefix is not stable.
  6. Apply the levers in order, one at a time, measuring steps per run and input tokens per run (median and p95) before and after; the tail is where loops live.
  7. Put budgets in the runtime: step, cost and wall-clock budgets checked every iteration, with a partial result and an explicit status when one trips (card 8).
  8. Report unit cost with volume beside it, by product and route, monthly; put cost per task next to quality in CI.

Token pitfalls

  • Instrumentation that stores only input and output tokens. It cannot explain the bill: it misses the cache discount on one side and the thinking surcharge on the other.
  • Dynamic content early in the prompt. A date, a user id, a request id in the system prompt: every call is a cache miss.
  • Capping output to control thinking. The thinking is billed anyway and the answer is cut off; lower the thinking level instead.
  • Tool results pasted whole into history. Paid on every later turn; summarise, truncate, or store and reference.
  • Sub-agents that inherit the full history. Multiplies the quadratic cost by the number of sub-agents; give them fresh windows.
  • Routing without an eval set. Cheap and wrong is not cheaper; measure quality per route.
  • Provisioned capacity bought for a launch. Idle capacity is the most expensive token; commit after utilisation is proven.
  • Price tables that are not versioned. Announced price changes and long-context surcharges belong in the budget with a date; a model switched silently breaks the trend.
  • A provider switch justified on list prices. Tokenisers differ, caching behaves differently, routing changes the mix: validate on cost per successful task with the eval set as the gate.

Training cost is a calculation, not a quote

FLOPs ≈ 6 × N_params × D_tokens GPU-hours = FLOPs ÷ (peak FLOP/s × MFU × 3,600) cost = GPU-hours × rate ÷ (1 − preemption overhead)

Model FLOPs utilisation (MFU) is the share of peak arithmetic the run achieves; 35–45% is good for large transformer training, far lower for small jobs, poorly tuned data loaders or oversized clusters. For serving, watch SM utilisation and memory per GPU (DCGM metrics in Cloud Monitoring): a fleet at 20% SM utilisation is paying for five GPUs to do one GPU’s work — consolidate with batching, multi-instance GPU (MIG) or time-slicing on GKE, and right-size the accelerator (an L4 serves many models an A100 was bought for). Managed tuning on Vertex AI is priced per training token, which turns the same calculation into tokens × rate.

Fine-tune or prompt?

break-even volume V* = (tuning cost + serving premium per month) ÷ (saving per request from a shorter prompt or a smaller model)

A tuned small model that replaces a long few-shot prompt on a frontier model saves on every request; the fixed cost is the tuning run, the data (card 10 of the Toloka playbook: annotation is priced per accepted item with QA inside), and the eval that proves quality held. Below V* requests a month, prompt; above it, tune; re-run the calculation when prices change, which they do.

Retrieval, runtimes, evaluation, data

Retrieval is billed by embedding tokens at ingestion, by index serving nodes or queries at run time (vector search nodes, Vertex AI Search per thousand queries, RAG Engine components), and by storage; its waste is re-embedding unchanged chunks and indexes sized for a launch. Agent Runtime on the Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform bills vCPU-hours and GiB-hours while agents are deployed, so an idle agent costs; Cloud Run tools bill per request and instance time. Evaluation costs judge calls per item — run on batch tiers, with a cheap judge where the rubric allows (the rubrics primer), and a budget as a share of inference. Data is cost of goods: a reasoning item at 50% yield costs twice its labour per accepted item; a preference pair carries goldens, adjudication and platform overhead on top of labour — the scoping playbook’s unit economics, which belong in the model’s cost per unit of value once the model is trained on them.

Managing the stack

  1. Give every layer a unit and an owner: accelerator-hours (platform), training tokens (ML), tokens by kind (product), vector nodes and queries (search), vCPU/GiB-hours (agents), judge calls (quality), accepted items (data).
  2. Put utilisation next to cost: MFU per training run, SM and memory utilisation per serving fleet, tokens per provisioned unit, queries per index node, agent hours with zero requests.
  3. Move work to the right price model: Spot with checkpointing for anything restartable, flex-start for anything that can wait, reservations and CUDs for the proven floor, on-demand for the rest.
  4. Right-size the accelerator per model: measure memory and throughput, consolidate with batching, MIG or time-slicing, and keep the big GPUs for the models that need them.
  5. Decide tune-vs-prompt with the break-even, including data and eval cost, and re-decide on price changes.
  6. Run evaluation on batch tiers with the cheapest judge the rubric allows; budget evals as a share of inference and report it.
  7. Retrieval hygiene: embed once, index to the traffic, scale nodes with queries, delete abandoned indexes.
  8. Count the data: cost per accepted item with yield and QA, carried into the unit economics of the trained model.

Platform pitfalls

  • GPUs bought for the launch. A fleet sized for projected traffic at 20% utilisation for a year; commit to the proven floor only.
  • Training on on-demand with no checkpoints. Spot would have been a third of the price; a preemption without checkpoints restarts from zero.
  • MFU never measured. A 15% MFU run costs three times a 45% one for the same model.
  • Indexes nobody queries. Vector serving nodes bill while idle; so do deployed agents.
  • Evaluation on the frontier tier. Nightly evals on the most expensive model with a 1–10 holistic judge, interactive priced.
  • Tune-vs-prompt decided on inference alone. Data, eval and the serving premium are the fixed cost; they decide the break-even.
  • Data cost left out of the model’s unit economics. The annotation programme that made the model is part of its cost per unit of value.
  • Region-bound GPU commitments and a capacity move. Reservations do not follow the workload; check capacity before committing.

# platforms/estimate.py — the calculation Finance can audit
def training_cost(params: float, tokens: float, peak_flops: float, mfu: float, rate_per_hour: float,
                  spot_discount: float = 0.0, preemption_overhead: float = 0.0) -> dict:
    flops = 6 * params * tokens
    gpu_hours = flops / (peak_flops * mfu * 3600)
    effective_rate = rate_per_hour * (1 - spot_discount)
    cost = gpu_hours * effective_rate / (1 - preemption_overhead)
    return {"flops": flops, "gpu_hours": round(gpu_hours, 1), "cost_usd": round(cost, 2)}

Setting it up

  1. Turn on the native detectors on every cloud and route them into one queue with the warehouse and observability detectors; one owner per account or project from the mapping.
  2. Write the warehouse detector on effective cost and on unit cost, same-weekday baselines, MAD-based spread, the three-condition trigger, plus a weekly-aggregate variant and a run-rate test (daily excess × 30 above monthly materiality) for slow leaks.
  3. Define severities and SLAs by projected monthly impact as a share of spend; put escalation paths and the Finance notification rule in writing.
  4. Triage with the change calendar and the data-lag rule before anyone is paged; log reason codes; feed accepted events into the forecast.

Anomaly and guardrail pitfalls

  • Alerts mistaken for controls. The email arrives after the money is spent; put a cap where the spend happens.
  • Mondays compared with weekends. The alert that fires every Monday on the same account; use same-weekday baselines, and give a weekly job its own threshold or a calendar entry — never mute the series.
  • Daily thresholds for slow leaks. A $13k-a-month drift never crosses $500 a day and a six-week baseline absorbs a step in three weeks; weekly aggregates and the savings tracker catch it.
  • “It’s growth” without a driver. Ask what moved; accept into the forecast only with a run-rate and a date.
  • Shared-commitment shifts paged as anomalies. When one account’s usage jumps, others’ amortised rate rises with no change in usage; the triage rule knows this.
  • Budgets per billing account only. Fifty thousand budgets are allowed; use project, folder, service and label scopes so the owner is the one alerted.
  • Quotas raised “temporarily”. With no expiry, a temporary quota is a permanent ceiling at the wrong height.
  • Runtime budgets in the prompt. “Do not use more than ten steps” is advice; the loop counter in the runtime is a control.

9 · Forecasting, budgeting & variance: three numbers, three jobs, one bridge

Actual is what happened; the forecast is what you now expect, remade every month with its drift logged; the budget is the plan, fixed for the year, never edited. How the forecast is built

  • Statistical baseline per series (service × account): a weekday profile, a median level, a damped and clipped trend, yearly seasonality only with thirteen months or more, storage on a constant-month basis because it is billed per GB-month. Medians so that one step does not become a trend.
  • Driver layer: unit cost × volume where a business driver exists (transactions, customers, tokens per conversation × conversations), from Business Planning’s volumes, not from the trend.
  • Known-changes register: migrations with ramp, double-running and switch-off dates; launches; commitment expiries and renewals; credits ending; announced price changes — each with an owner and a date, checked monthly. The switch-off date is usually the most valuable single field (a one-month slip in a migration can cost $200k).
  • One-time items and pipeline savings outside the modelled series: the renewal on its date; each initiative’s saving at its probability and start date.
  • AI spend as volume × tokens per unit × price per token by model and route — the risk is not growth but a release that changes tokens per conversation, which only a unit-cost gate catches.
  • A range from the backtest’s error distribution, quoted as a band (“±2.5% covers about 80% of months”), not a false point.

Accuracy

FAR = (F − A) ÷ F drift = (new F − previous F) ÷ previous F MAPE = mean |A−F| ÷ A WAPE = Σ|A−F| ÷ ΣA bias = Σ(F−A) ÷ ΣA MASE = MAE ÷ MAE of seasonal naive

WAPE weights errors by spend, which is what finance feels: the headline. MAPE weights scopes equally and finds the badly forecast small ones, but is undefined at zero and asymmetric. Bias shows direction: always a little high is padding, always a little low is optimism, and either fix is in the process. Total accuracy hides offsetting errors — a total within 1.6% while each cloud missed by about 5% in different directions — so measure where you manage the work. Backtest with a rolling origin (rebuild each month’s forecast from the data and register as they stood), and if seasonal naive does as well, the model is not earning its complexity.

Variance decomposition

with L = usage at list, e = effective cost per list dollar: volume = (ΣL_a − ΣL_f) × r̄_f mix = Σ(L_a·e_f) − ΣL_a·r̄_f rate = ΣL_a·(e_a − e_f) + one-time + timing, as separate lines

Reason codes on every variance line and every forecast miss — Timing, Usage, Rate (prices, discounts, commitments, FX), Mix, One-time, Data, Model — because after a few quarters the codes show where to invest; it is usually timing from the known-changes process, not the model.

Signal or noise

Normalise per day and weekday (and storage for month length); require persistence (three consecutive days or five of seven at daily level; two consecutive months at monthly); check breadth (one SKU or many), driver backing, the change calendar and data completeness; compare with the same-weekday median and a robust spread. Then pick the queue: anomaly ticket, forecast change, or nothing. A 9% drop in February is mostly three fewer days.

The monthly forecast procedure

  1. Basis and calendar agreed with Finance: amortised, fiscal months, budget FX, their cost-centre structure, their volumes as drivers; monthly refresh, quarterly reforecast, annual plan two to four months ahead.
  2. Rebuild the baseline per series on data through BD10; medians, damped trend, weekday profile, constant-month storage.
  3. Walk the known-changes register with owners: dates moved? completion, not plan.
  4. Apply drivers and one-time items; add pipeline savings at probability; put announced price changes on their dates.
  5. Backtest and set the range from the last twelve months’ rolling-origin errors; compare with seasonal naive.
  6. Write the accuracy log for the month just closed: forecast version, actual, FAR, drivers, reason codes, action.
  7. Decompose the variance against budget and against forecast; a bridge of at most six drivers that sums; commentary for an executive.
  8. Reconcile with the central team on a fixed business day before either side publishes; one owner per number; a reconciliation line in every report.
  9. Change the forecast only for persistent, explained changes; log the drift; never change the budget.

Forecasting pitfalls

  • Extrapolating a migration ramp or a one-off. A mean-based trend turns one step into a slope; medians, clipping and a register entry prevent it.
  • Forecasting AI as a dollar trend. Volume × tokens per unit × price, by route; a release can change tokens per conversation by 30% overnight.
  • A point with no range. Finance hears a promise; quote the band from the backtest.
  • Accuracy on the total only. Offsetting errors look like skill.
  • Editing the budget. The budget measures the plan; the forecast measures what you know now.
  • Missing switch-off dates. The most expensive field in the register when it is blank.
  • Two teams, two forecasts. Without a signed single-source-of-truth agreement the meeting is a reconciliation, not a decision.
  • Changing the forecast on one high week. Persistence first; log every change with its reason and size.

# forecast/model.py — a transparent forecast: median baseline with weekday profile and damped trend, plus a driver layer
import numpy as np, pandas as pd

def baseline(daily: pd.Series, horizon_days: int, damp: float = 0.8, clip: float = 0.03) -> pd.Series:
    """daily effective cost for one series (service × account); medians so steps do not become trends."""
    last6 = daily.last("42D")
    weekday_profile = last6.groupby(last6.index.dayofweek).median() / last6.median()
    monthly = daily.resample("MS").sum()
    growth = np.clip(np.median(monthly.pct_change().dropna().tail(6)), -clip, clip)   # clipped monthly growth, median of 6
    level = last6.median()
    idx = pd.date_range(daily.index[-1] + pd.Timedelta(days=1), periods=horizon_days)
    months_ahead = ((idx - daily.index[-1]).days / 30.4)
    trend = (1 + growth * damp) ** months_ahead                                 # damped
    return pd.Series(level * weekday_profile.reindex(idx.dayofweek).to_numpy() * trend, index=idx)

def driver_layer(volumes: pd.Series, unit_cost: float) -> pd.Series:        # e.g. conversations × tokens/conv × $/token
    return volumes * unit_cost

def known_changes(idx: pd.DatetimeIndex, register: list[dict]) -> pd.Series:
    """register rows: {start, end, daily_delta, ramp_days, probability}; migrations, launches, expiries, price changes."""
    out = pd.Series(0.0, index=idx)
    for r in register:
        days = idx[(idx >= r["start"]) & (idx <= r.get("end", idx[-1]))]
        ramp = np.minimum(1.0, (days - pd.Timestamp(r["start"])).days / max(r.get("ramp_days", 1), 1))
        out.loc[days] += r["daily_delta"] * ramp * r.get("probability", 1.0)
    return out

def accuracy(F: pd.Series, A: pd.Series) -> dict:
    err = (A - F).abs()
    naive = (A - A.shift(12)).abs().dropna()                                     # seasonal naive at a 12-month lag
    return {"FAR": float((F.sum() - A.sum()) / F.sum()), "WAPE": float(err.sum() / A.sum()),
            "MAPE": float((err / A).mean()), "bias": float((F - A).sum() / A.sum()),
            "MASE": float(err.mean() / naive.mean()) if len(naive) else None}

def decompose(L_a, L_f, e_a, e_f):
    """per series arrays: L = usage at list, e = effective cost per list dollar; returns volume, mix, rate."""
    r_f = (L_f * e_f).sum() / L_f.sum()                                          # average forecast rate
    volume = (L_a.sum() - L_f.sum()) * r_f
    mix = (L_a * e_f).sum() - L_a.sum() * r_f
    rate = (L_a * (e_a - e_f)).sum()
    return {"volume": volume, "mix": mix, "rate": rate, "check": volume + mix + rate - ((L_a * e_a).sum() - (L_f * e_f).sum())}

Measured from the usage object on every call, priced from a versioned table, capped in the gateway and the runtime, reported as cost per resolved conversation next to quality.

The long form. I instrument first: every model call is a span with the four token counts, the model, the step and the preceding tool, priced from a versioned table and reconciled to the provider bill monthly; every tool records its output size so I can compute the carry cost — tokens a tool introduced times the turns that followed. Then I read the ten most expensive traces of the day; they explain more than any dashboard. The levers go in order of leverage: caching, which makes the repeated prefix nearly free and turns a 162,500-token ten-turn run into the equivalent of about 30,000; routing, which needs the eval set; context engineering, which shrinks the growth; thinking levels per step, because reasoning is billed as output whether you see it or not; batch for evals and backfills at half price. Around the product: runtime budgets per run, gateway caps per key and tenant, project quotas, and spend-cap budgets where the platform has them, because a loop can spend the month before the billing export lands. On Google Cloud that is Gemini’s implicit caching and explicit cache objects, thinking levels, the batch tier, provisioned throughput for the proven floor, the gen-AI Flexible Savings Plan for the spend floor, Agent Runtime’s vCPU and memory hours, the FinOps Explainability Agent for cost by model, key and token type, and early anomalies for AI services at twenty to forty minutes. Beneath inference, the rest of the stack — accelerators on the right price model with checkpoints on Spot, MFU on training runs, retrieval that scales with queries, evaluation on batch tiers, and the data with its yield — each with a unit, an owner and a monthly number. The report is unit cost next to quality, by route, with volume beside it.

Questions they ask, and the shape of the answer

  • “Walk me through a 12-month forecast across three clouds.”Amortised by charge date per service and account; statistical baseline per series (weekday profile, median level, damped clipped trend, seasonality only with ≥ 13 months, storage constant-month); driver layer from the known-changes register; one-time items; pipeline savings at probability; rolling-origin backtest; a range from the errors. (Card 9)
  • “MAPE, WAPE or bias?”All three — dollars, scopes, direction — plus MASE against naive; and measure where you manage the work, because totals hide offsetting errors. (Card 9)
  • “September is 19% over budget. Explain it in two minutes.”Timing $117k (migration early, ends 1 Nov), one-time $48k, rate $23k, AI growth $25k, offsets −$15k; against forecast $73k, all off the change calendar; the year lands $430k over; two decisions recover $40k a month. (Card 9)
  • “Which cost number do you use?”Billed for invoices and cash, effective for showback, trends and forecasts; Google cost plus credits; FOCUS’s four columns across clouds; never sum list over purchases and the usage they cover. (Card 2)
  • “Design a tagging standard for three clouds.”Four keys in lowercase kebab-case, catalogue values, two derived from application, enforced in IaC, compliance by cost; hierarchy before tags. (Card 3)
  • “How do you allocate shared costs without disputes?”Few named pools, a written method each agreed before the numbers, shared cost on its own line, rule changes at fiscal boundaries, a dispute window with materiality. (Card 3)
  • “How do you size a commitment?”Hourly eligible usage after removing what leaves; break-even 1 − d; the (1 − d) quantile; the plateau’s lower edge; three years for the floor, one for the next layer; ladder. (Card 4)
  • “Coverage is 56% and utilisation 100%. Buy more?”Not before checking what is leaving; the plan expiring in January and the data platform switching off in November change the answer to a renewal at $210 an hour, not more now. (Card 4)
  • “Your program realised 85% of plan. Good or bad?”Depends on which initiative decayed and why — the scheduling opt-outs without expiry — and on the method per initiative; report realisation per initiative and the pipeline by stage. (Card 5)
  • “How do you forecast AI spend?”Volume × tokens per unit × price per token by model and route; the risk is a release that changes tokens per conversation, which only a unit-cost gate catches. (Cards 6, 9)
  • “Why did the bill double with no traffic change?”A reasoning model: thinking is billed as output; four times the visible answer in the worked turn. Thinking levels per step, not per agent. (Card 6)
  • “How do you stop an agent from spending the budget?”Runtime budgets on steps, dollars and seconds; gateway caps per key; quotas; spend-cap budgets; observability-side detection in minutes — the enforcement ladder, because an alert arrives after the money. (Card 8)
  • “A team wants to switch provider to save money.”Validate on cost per successful task with the eval set as the gate; tokenisers, caching and routing differ; count the one-time work and idle provisioned capacity left behind. (Cards 6, 10)
  • “Tune or prompt?”Break-even volume = fixed cost (tuning, data with QA, eval, serving premium) ÷ saving per request; re-run on price changes. (Card 7)
  • “What goes on your one page?”Three numbers with comparisons, a six-driver bridge that sums, outlook with a range, four sized risks, opportunities by stage, decisions with owners and dates, two lines of method and reconciliation. (Card 10)
  • “How do you build credibility with engineers who see FinOps as a tax?”Their units, reproducible evidence, no surprises in front of their leadership, help with the tedious part, public credit. (Card 10)
