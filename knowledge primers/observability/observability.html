1 · Observability as a discipline: the signals, the questions, the program

Monitoring answers the questions you knew to ask; observability lets you explain any internal state, including the ones nobody anticipated, from data the system already emits, without shipping new code. The word is Kálmán’s (1960): a system is observable when its state can be inferred from its outputs. The job in 2026 is three jobs at once: run a telemetry platform as a product, define what “working” means for users and turn it into SLOs and contracts, and set a direction that forty teams adopt.

Two public incidents every observability engineer should know

  • OpenAI, 11 December 2024. A new telemetry service for Kubernetes metrics made every node run API operations whose cost grew with cluster size; the API servers were overwhelmed, DNS-based discovery broke, and the API, ChatGPT and Sora were down or degraded for about 4 h 20 min. Staging did not reproduce it (only large clusters), DNS caching delayed the symptoms until the rollout had reached every cluster, and the fix needed the overloaded control plane. Lessons: telemetry agents have the widest footprint of anything you deploy; roll them out in phases with load gates; keep break-glass access that does not depend on what is failing.
  • Datadog, 8 March 2023. An automatic systemd update restarted the network daemon and deleted the routes the container network relied on, on tens of thousands of nodes across five regions; about 27 hours of lost or degraded dashboards, alerting and history. Datadog’s own conclusion: live data and alerts matter far more than history. For customers: your monitoring vendor has an availability of its own, so keep a minimal independent paging path for your most important SLOs.

Running observability as a program

Six questions to ask of any component

  1. What grows with traffic and what grows with cardinality? Volume drives samples, bytes and spans; distinct label values drive series, index size and memory. They grow independently, and the second one surprises people.
  2. What is shared? An ingester, a Kafka partition, a query frontend, a Collector gateway or an Alertmanager is the blast radius of one bad tenant, query or deploy.
  3. What is retried, and by whom? Retries multiply load when the system is weakest; telemetry clients retry too, and a retry after a timeout can write twice.
  4. What is unbounded? Queues, label values, query ranges, log line sizes, trace durations, fan-out. Each needs a limit and a behaviour at the limit.
  5. What depends on what? Draw the graph and look for cycles: does the tool you need during an outage depend on the system that is out?
  6. What changes over time? Every metric, dashboard, alert, service and convention has a life cycle; most production telemetry problems are changes nobody planned.

The strategy on one page: principles that hold up

  • OpenTelemetry for all new instrumentation; vendor-neutral by default, so backends can change without re-instrumenting.
  • Every tier-1 service has SLOs and burn-rate alerts; a page without an SLO or a named failure mode behind it needs a written justification.
  • Telemetry has a budget: every team sees its active series, ingested gigabytes and cost.
  • Dashboards, alerts, SLOs and Collector pipelines are code: reviewed, owned, versioned.
  • High-cardinality context lives in traces and logs; metrics carry bounded aggregates.
  • The monitoring path is in a different failure domain from what it monitors.

Where programs go wrong

  • Paging on causes. CPU at 80%, a pod restart, a queue depth: pages that need no action train people to ignore the pager. Page on SLO burn; make cause alerts tickets.
  • Metrics with identifiers. A user id, request id, full URL or error text as a metric label multiplies series until the backend falls over. Identifiers belong on spans and logs.
  • Monitoring that depends on what it monitors. Prometheus on the cluster it watches, paging through the same SSO and DNS, runbooks behind the failing login. Separate failure domains and an external dead-man’s switch.
  • Means of heavy-tailed things. MTTR as a mean is moved by one long incident; Google’s Incident Metrics in SRE (2021) argues these means are poor for decisions, and DORA replaced “mean time to restore” with failed-deployment recovery time. Report medians and percentiles.
  • “Alert reduction” as the success metric. Easy to achieve by suppressing alerts; the metric that matters is time to detect and mitigate at a given precision.
  • Dashboards nobody opens and metrics nobody queries. Usage data (mimirtool analysis, query logs, Adaptive Metrics) tells you what to delete; a metric nobody queries is pure cost.
  • Averages for latency. 980 requests at 100 ms, 15 at 1 s, 5 at 10 s: the mean is 163 ms, the p99.9 is 10 s, and no user waited 163 ms.
  • Telemetry rolled out like an app. Agents and Collectors touch every node; a bad configuration reaches everything at once (OpenAI, December 2024). Phased rollouts with load gates, tested at production cluster sizes.
  • Retiring half an object. Removing a service without its scrape targets, alerts and SLOs leaves absence alerts paging forever; renaming a metric without dual emission breaks every dashboard that looks back.

Why averages lie, in one row of numbers

1,000 requests: 980 at 100 ms, 15 at 1 s, 5 at 10 s. Mean = (980 × 0.1 + 15 × 1 + 5 × 10) / 1,000 = 0.163 s; median 100 ms; p99 1 s; p99.9 10 s. Five users in a thousand waited a hundred times longer than the median and the mean does not show it. An agent run is slow if any one of its calls is slow, which makes this worse (card 7).

Instrumenting a service so the data is right

  1. Identity in the SDK. OTEL_SERVICE_NAME and OTEL_RESOURCE_ATTRIBUTES=service.namespace=support,service.version=2.3.0,deployment.environment.name=production; let the node agent add Kubernetes and cloud attributes. Declarative configuration (OTEL_CONFIG_FILE, stable March 2026) replaces dozens of variables where the language supports it.
  2. Three layers of instrumentation. Auto (the Java agent, opentelemetry-instrument, the Node register hook) for HTTP, gRPC, database and messaging spans; manual spans and attributes for what the business and the SLOs need; eBPF (OBI) for binaries you cannot change. Libraries depend on the API only; the application configures the SDK once.
  3. Name spans for grouping. {method} {route}, chat {model}, execute_tool {tool}: never an id in the name, or span metrics and dashboards fragment.
  4. Set kind and status by the rules. CLIENT for a remote call, SERVER for handling one, PRODUCER/CONSUMER around queues with links for batches, INTERNAL otherwise. Error only when the operation failed: a server span for a 404 stays Unset, the client span that received it is Error; a cancellation the caller intended is not an error.
  5. Propagate across every boundary. HTTP headers by default (tracecontext,baggage); message headers on produce and extract on consume; thread pools and async callbacks that lose context; proxies that strip unknown headers; serverless triggers; MCP _meta. Track the share of broken traces as a platform metric.
  6. Choose instruments by aggregation. Histograms for anything you will quantile or aggregate, with buckets that bracket SLO thresholds; counters for totals; gauges only for values that must not be summed. Cap cardinality with Views (attribute allow-lists).
  7. Pin and translate conventions. Record the schema URL on scope; migrate with dual emission (/dup), the Collector’s schema processor or OTTL renames, query-time unions, and warehouse views; wait one retention period before removing old names.
  8. Watch what the SDK drops. Attributes beyond the default limit of 128 vanish silently; alert on dropped_attributes_count; keep large content out of attributes (card 3).

What people get wrong about spans and metrics

  • Ids in span names. GET /orders/8713 makes every request its own operation; use GET /orders/{id} (the route) and put the id in an attribute.
  • Error on 4xx server spans, Ok set by libraries. A client mistake is not a server failure; Ok cannot be changed back once set and is reserved for application overrides.
  • Links added after creation. Head samplers decide at span start from what they see; a link added later never influences the decision.
  • Latency from two clocks. A child that starts before its parent is clock skew; compute cross-host latency from each side’s measured durations, run chrony everywhere, alert on offset.
  • Delta into a cumulative backend. Prometheus drops deltas unless converted (deltatocumulative processor or an experimental flag); the SDK default is cumulative, keep it.
  • Summaries where you need fleet quantiles. Client-side quantiles cannot be merged across instances; use histograms.
  • Baggage with personal data. It travels to every downstream service, including third parties, and appears on spans only if a processor copies it. Hash user identifiers.
  • Exceptions as span events. That form is deprecated in favour of log-based events correlated with the span; keep exception.type, .message, .stacktrace as the attribute names.
  • Old and new names mixed. http.server.duration in milliseconds and http.server.request.duration in seconds in one dashboard; gen_ai.system beside gen_ai.provider.name. Translate in the Collector and union at query time until the old data ages out.
  • Protocol and port mismatch at first setup. The spec default is http/protobuf on 4318; some agents default to gRPC on 4317; a mismatch fails quietly.

# a manual span with the right kind, status and attributes; errors recorded, Ok never set
from opentelemetry.trace import SpanKind, Status, StatusCode
import time

def run_tool(name, fn, **args):
    with tracer.start_as_current_span(f"execute_tool {name}", kind=SpanKind.INTERNAL) as span:
        span.set_attribute("gen_ai.operation.name", "execute_tool")
        span.set_attribute("gen_ai.tool.name", name)
        t0 = time.perf_counter()
        try:
            result = fn(**args)
            span.set_attribute("acme.tool.result.bytes", len(result.encode()) if isinstance(result, str) else -1)
            tool_duration.record(time.perf_counter() - t0, {"gen_ai.tool.name": name})
            return result
        except Exception as e:                                   # typed exceptions: never a string "Error: …"
            span.set_status(Status(StatusCode.ERROR, str(e)))
            span.set_attribute("error.type", type(e).__name__)
            tool_duration.record(time.perf_counter() - t0, {"gen_ai.tool.name": name, "error.type": type(e).__name__})
            raise

Building it, and reading it before touching the prompt

Three layers of instrumentation

  1. Automatic. The model SDK and framework instrumentations (opentelemetry-instrumentation-google-genai, -vertexai, -anthropic, -openai; ADK’s built-in tracing; Langfuse’s and LangSmith’s OpenTelemetry-based SDKs) give model-call spans with usage for free.
  2. Explicit spans around the loop and each tool, which give the tree its shape: an invoke_agent root per turn or run with gen_ai.agent.name and gen_ai.conversation.id; an execute_tool span per tool with result size; a retrieval span with index, top_k and returned ids; sub-agents as child invoke_agent spans.
  3. What the automatic layer cannot know: tool result sizes, budget breaches, compaction points, prompt version, side-effect and approval flags, the cost you computed, the tenant and feature from baggage.
  4. Content policy per data tier. Off by default; span_only, event_only or span_and_event with gen_ai_latest_experimental; or OTEL_INSTRUMENTATION_GENAI_COMPLETION_HOOK=upload with an fsspec path (a bucket) so content goes to storage, not telemetry. Redact in the Collector before export; hash identifiers; short retention and access logging for content.
  5. Export. OTLP to a node Collector, which adds Kubernetes attributes, derives RED and cost metrics before sampling, and routes to Cloud Trace, Tempo, Langfuse or all of them. The choice of backend is a configuration of exporters, not a rewrite.

“The dataset exists, the agent says it does not”: six checks, in order

  1. Did it call the tool at all? No execute_tool span for the lookup means the model guessed. Fix the tool description (the only source of truth), forbid answering without it, force a tool call on the first step where it matters, trim a thirty-tool list, add the case to the eval set.
  2. Did the tool fail, and how? error.type on the span: a 403 is identity (service account, dataset IAM, VPC Service Controls); a 404 is the address (project, location, id).
  3. Were the arguments right? The span’s input: display name instead of id, Finance_2026 for finance_2026, wrong project or region. Durable fix: a resolver tool that returns candidate ids so the lookup is called with an id the system produced.
  4. Was the result empty or cut off? First page of fifty when the dataset is the sixty-first; truncation by the wrapper; an empty list where an exception was due. Record output size on every tool span.
  5. Was the error swallowed? A wrapper that returns "Error: dataset not found" as a string produces a span with status OK and a confident wrong answer. Tools raise typed exceptions; the message to the model distinguishes “lookup failed” from “found nothing”.
  6. Was the result stale? A cached listing from before the dataset existed; a cache key without the identity. Then, and only then, the prompt — usually by reducing what the model had to read.

Where LLM telemetry goes wrong

  • Provider semantics copied straight into the conventions. Anthropic’s usage.input_tokens counts only tokens after the last cache breakpoint; the conventions’ count is the total. A library that copies the field makes uncached input too small, even negative. Check one span per library against the raw usage object, clamp, alert.
  • Summing usage over every span of a trace. invoke_agent may repeat the totals; reasoning tokens are already inside output. Sum inference spans only; never add reasoning again.
  • Content in span attributes. Prompts blow the attribute limits and put personal data in the trace store with long retention; use events or the upload hook, redact in the Collector, keep retention short.
  • Conversation or user id as a metric label. A span attribute, never a label (card 4 prices it).
  • Mixed names from different library versions. gen_ai.system vs gen_ai.provider.name, prompt_tokens vs input_tokens, cache_creation vs cache_write; and there is no schema URL for GenAI yet. Normalise with OTTL in the Collector (cookbook).
  • Status OK with an error string. The signature of a swallowed exception: a successful tool call followed by a confident wrong answer.
  • Retries after the first streamed token. Visible to the user; retry only before the first token, and count each attempt as its own span.
  • Time to first API token mistaken for time to first visible token. The first token may belong to a hidden reasoning or tool step; measure visibility at the client.
  • Turning on capture with the wrong flag. On Google Cloud, OTEL_INSTRUMENTATION_GENAI_CAPTURE_MESSAGE_CONTENT=true together with gen_ai_latest_experimental is an invalid combination and nothing is collected; use EVENT_ONLY (card 10).

2 · Sizing tail sampling for 50,000 spans per second

At 25 spans per trace that is 2,000 new traces per second. With a 30-second decision wait the tier holds 60,000 traces, about 1.5 GB at 1 KB per span, spread over the replicas; set num_traces above the held count, give the pods headroom, put memory_limiter at about 75% of the limit, and plan for trace splitting when the tier scales. For a larger estate: 20,000 traces per second over four replicas is 5,000 × 30 = 150,000 traces per replica, about 3 GB at 20 spans per trace, so num_traces 200,000 and 6 GB pods. Content-heavy spans can be ten times larger; measure your own.

3 · What tail sampling does to a mean

100,000 runs: 95,000 ordinary at $0.03 and 5,000 expensive at $0.30. A policy that keeps every expensive run and 5% of the rest stores 4,750 + 5,000 = 9,750 traces. Their unweighted mean is (4,750 × 0.03 + 5,000 × 0.30) / 9,750 = $0.168, almost four times the true $0.0435; their total, $1,642.50, is 38% of the true $4,350. Weighting the ordinary traces by 20 (their adjusted count) restores the true mean. Card 8 draws it.

Designing the pipeline

  1. Identity in the SDK, infrastructure in the agent. Service name, namespace, version and environment where they cannot be wrong; Kubernetes and cloud attributes where they are cheap.
  2. Agent per node, gateway for whole-trace work. Applications export to the node-local agent (OTEL_EXPORTER_OTLP_ENDPOINT=http://$(HOST_IP):4318); the gateway does tail sampling, redaction, span metrics, tenant routing and egress with credentials in one place.
  3. Order the processors. memory_limiter first; transforms (name normalisation, redaction, cost) before anything derives metrics; span_metrics before tail_sampling; batch last.
  4. Choose sampling by outcome. Keep all traces with an Error span, above the latency SLO, above a cost threshold, and a 5% baseline; write the applied probability into tracestate; export inference spans unsampled to the warehouse without content.
  5. Budget cardinality. A series budget per team; a CI lint that rejects user_id, request_id, email, raw URLs and conversation ids as metric attributes; SDK Views with allow-lists; sample_limit per scrape; per-tenant limits in Mimir.
  6. Monitor the monitoring. A watchdog alert that always fires through an external dead-man’s switch; absence alerts on every SLI input; heartbeat series for push pipelines; the Collector’s refused, failed and queue metrics; remote-write lag alerts well inside the WAL window; a meta-monitoring stack in another failure domain.
  7. Model capacity. Active series, samples per second, GB per day, spans per second, traces held in tail sampling, and the cost of each; what breaks first at 10×.
  8. Validate and roll out like production code. otelcol-contrib validate --config in CI; phased rollouts of agents with cluster-health gates and production-size tests; a deprecation calendar (Grafana Agent EOL Nov 2025, Promtail EOL Mar 2026, Tempo 3 architecture, Collector renames).

Pipeline mistakes

  • Two writers for one series. Gateway replicas routed by trace id each compute the same span-metric series; Prometheus and Mimir keep only job and instance from the resource, so streams collide, samples are rejected as duplicates or counters seem to reset. Compute in the node agent or a service-routed tier.
  • Batching before sampling, no memory limiter. Bursts, large batches or a slow exporter push the Collector past its limit; an OOM kill loses buffered data.
  • Head sampling and then computing SLIs from traces. A 10% head sample stores a tenth of the errors and the SLI is noise; derive SLIs from unsampled metrics.
  • Scaling the tail-sampling tier with an aggressive autoscaler. Every scaling event reroutes a share of in-flight traces and splits them; use a StatefulSet or long stabilisation windows.
  • labeldrop that merges series. If two series differ only by the dropped label they collapse into one series with two writers and Prometheus rejects the duplicates.
  • Old component names after the 2026 renames. otlphttp → otlp_http, spanmetrics → span_metrics, k8sattributes → k8s_attributes; aliases log warnings, and dynamic_sampling → adaptive_tail_sampling kept no alias. Much vendor documentation still shows the old names.
  • Debug logs and health checks shipped to the backend and filtered there, paying ingestion for nothing. Drop them at the agent.
  • Remote write that falls behind quietly. The WAL covers about two hours; a destination down longer than that loses data. Alert on the gap between highest and highest-sent timestamps.
  • A Collector tier nobody monitors. It fails silently; refused and failed-send counters and queue size are the three metrics that tell you.

Capacity arithmetic

One million active series scraped every 15 s is 66,700 samples per second; at 1.5 bytes per sample that is about 130 GB for 15 days of retention. The same million series need memory in the head proportional to series and churn, which is why a pod-name label on a high-volume metric costs more than the samples do.

Metrics hygiene, as a review

  1. Review every new metric for name (snake_case, unit suffix, _total for counters, or the OTLP name with its unit), type, labels (bounded, on the allow-list), a cardinality estimate and an owner.
  2. Buckets around the SLO thresholds, or exponential histograms; a threshold must be a boundary for a classic histogram, and le values differ between scraped (1.0) and OTLP-ingested (1) data, so list the label’s values before writing a selector.
  3. Record what dashboards and alerts evaluate repeatedly, at the level they use; never record unaggregated high-cardinality data.
  4. Write alerts with for and a runbook, a severity, and a link to the SLO or failure mode they protect; a watchdog alert that always fires through an external dead-man’s switch; inhibition so a cluster-down alert mutes its pod alerts.
  5. Contain cardinality at every layer: SDK Views, Collector transforms, sample_limit and label_limit per scrape, per-tenant limits in Mimir, a CI lint for identifiers in labels, a dashboard of series by team.
  6. Find offenders in this order: the TSDB status page (/api/v1/status/tsdb?limit=20), per-target scrape_series_added, targeted PromQL, promtool tsdb analyze offline.
  7. Run HA pairs with a replica label and deduplicate in Mimir or Thanos; point every Prometheus or ruler at every Alertmanager replica rather than a load balancer.
  8. Prune by usage: mimirtool analyze against dashboards and rules, query logs, Adaptive Metrics; delete what nobody queried in 90 days with a dual-emission window if it was renamed.

The thirteen PromQL mistakes

  • rate on a gauge, or deriv on a counter.
  • Summing counters before rate. One restart becomes a spike that never happened.
  • A rate window shorter than four scrape intervals. Gaps and empty results.
  • Averaging percentiles, or dropping le before histogram_quantile. A pod with 1% of traffic and a 2 s p99 averaged with one at 100 ms gives 1.05 s, the p99 of no population.
  • A hard-coded integer le. Scraped boundaries are le="1.0" in Prometheus 3, OTLP-ingested ones le="1".
  • Forgetting that comparisons filter. The ratio panel has holes; use bool when you want zeros.
  • Binary operations whose label sets do not match. They return nothing and raise no error.
  • sum(up) to find down targets. Use count by (job) (up == 0).
  • irate in alerts and recording rules.
  • Dividing by a zero rate. NaN or +Inf; filter the denominator or clamp.
  • Assuming a missing series means zero. Absent data needs absent_over_time or an or 0 * … fallback, or the alert never fires.
  • topk in range queries. Membership changes at every step; pin it with @ end().
  • Expensive regular expressions on high-cardinality labels in dashboards many people open; scope every selector with job or namespace.

6 · SLIs, SLOs, error budgets and alerting: paging on what users feel

An SLI is a measured ratio of good events to valid events; an SLO is a target for it over a window; the error budget is one minus the SLO, in events or minutes; an SLA is a contract with consequences, deliberately looser than the internal SLO. Detection times, worked

  • A total outage pushes the 1-hour ratio past 1.44% after 0.0144 × 60 ≈ 52 seconds and the 5-minute ratio in about 4 seconds: the page goes out within a minute or two once scrape and evaluation delays are added.
  • A 2% error ratio needs 0.0144 / 0.02 × 60 ≈ 43 minutes to move the 1-hour average past 1.44%; by then about 2% of the budget is gone, which is the design intent.
  • A 0.5% error ratio (burn 5) never pages. It opens a ticket after about 14.4 hours, when the 1-day ratio passes 0.3% and the 3-day ratio passes 0.1%, with 10% of the budget spent; left alone it exhausts the budget in six days. That gap is a deliberate trade between noise and speed, and you should be able to say so.

Composite availability

Serial dependencies multiply: an edge at 99.99%, checkout at 99.95%, its database at 99.95% and a payment provider at 99.9% give 0.9999 × 0.9995 × 0.9995 × 0.999 ≈ 99.79%, about 91 minutes of expected unavailability in a 30-day month, more than twice the 43 minutes a 99.9% SLO allows. You cannot promise 99.9% on top of that chain without changing it. Parallel replicas: two regions at 99.9% give 99.9999% on paper, but failures correlate and the failover path has its own availability, so with a failover at 99.95% the result is about 99.95% and the failover sets the ceiling. Four hard dependencies at 99.9% each: about 99.6% before your own failures.

What a canary can detect

With 5% of 100 requests per second, the canary sees 5 per second. Telling a 1% error ratio from a 0.1% baseline needs about 1,060 requests per arm, three to four minutes; telling 0.2% from 0.1% needs about 23,500 per arm, about 78 minutes (two-sided test at 5%, 80% power). A canary stops gross regressions within minutes, a 99% latency SLI catches a doubling with a tenth of the traffic because its bad events are ten times more common, and subtle regressions are left to burn-rate alerts after rollout. Annotate every deploy on the dashboards.

An error-budget policy, in one paragraph

Service: checkout. SLOs: 99.9% of valid requests succeed and 99% complete within 250 ms, measured at the edge over a rolling 30 days. Normal: above 25% of either budget, the usual release process. At risk: at 25% or less, or after any fast-burn page in the last seven days, each release needs a reliability review and a quarter of sprint capacity goes to reliability. Exhausted: feature releases stop until the 30-day SLI is back above target; security fixes and risk-reducing changes continue; postmortem actions become the top priority. Single large incident: more than 20% of a budget gets a postmortem within five business days. What counts: planned maintenance and dependency failures count, because users notice them. Disputes go to the engineering director; reviewed quarterly.

From user journeys to rules to contracts

  1. Start from journeys (“pay for an order”, “an alert reaches the on-call engineer”), not from the metrics you have; for each, what does the user notice when it breaks, and which SLI type fits.
  2. Write the specification and the implementation: “checkout requests that succeed” and “non-5xx responses at the edge load balancer, from its request counters”. Decide what “valid” excludes (malformed, unauthenticated, above contracted limits).
  3. Pick request-based or window-based; for low-traffic services the workbook’s remedies are synthetic traffic, combining services into one monitored unit, retries that absorb failures, or a looser SLO over a longer window.
  4. Set the target from measured performance and business need, stricter than anything sold: measured ≫ SLO for quarters means the SLO is too loose to guide decisions; measured < SLO means the system or the promise must change.
  5. Generate the rules (Sloth, Pyrra, OpenSLO) and write them by hand once so you can debug what the generators produce: 5-minute error and request rates, 30-day sums, budget remaining, multiwindow burn-rate alerts.
  6. Sign the error-budget policy with product and engineering, and put the SLI in the release path as a canary gate compared against the stable version.
  7. Give the platform its own SLOs, evaluated from a meta-monitoring stack: ingestion availability (99.9%), freshness and correctness by a write-and-read-back canary (99% of probes), query success within 10 s per tenant (99.5%), alert delivery within 2 minutes (99.9%); count wrong data as bad.
  8. Review SLAs you buy and sell in ten minutes: definitions before numbers; the measurement source and whether you can see the data; the target converted into minutes per month and per incident; every exclusion and how much of a real outage it removes; the credit for a bad month against your cost of that month; the claims deadline in a calendar; the promise against the provider’s architecture and your dependency chain; change rights and chronic-failure exits; the SLO you will monitor from your side; what you would do if they failed for a day.

Where SLOs and SLAs go hollow

  • Paging when the error rate exceeds 1% for five minutes. Short spikes page for nothing and slow leaks never page; the threshold ignores how much budget is at stake.
  • Counting 4xx as bad and 429 as good. One punishes client bugs, the other hides throttling. A 429 below the contracted limit is a failure; above it, it is not.
  • A latency threshold that is not a bucket boundary, or a p99 quoted from a window with too few requests.
  • An SLA at or above the SLO. No room to act before money moves; measurement and window mismatches need headroom.
  • Excluding dependency failures and maintenance from the budget. Users notice them; the response is to change the architecture or the dependency.
  • Calendar windows for engineering decisions. They reset on the first and invite spending the budget at month end.
  • Platform SLIs evaluated inside the platform. A Mimir outage hides its own SLI; a query that returns quickly with missing samples is worse than an error, so canaries check results, not status codes.
  • The hollow SLA. “Commercially reasonable efforts” as the whole promise; “available” undefined; measured only by the provider’s internal monitoring; open-ended or “emergency” maintenance; “degraded performance” excluded; a minimum outage length that swallows real outages (a 99.99% target allows 4.3 minutes, so excluding outages under 15 minutes makes a month of 14-minute outages every hour compliant); third-party failures excluded wholesale; credits “up to 5%” at the provider’s discretion; no claim process; the right to change the SLA at any time; a number the architecture cannot support.
  • Credits mistaken for insurance. 10% of a $20,000 monthly bill is $2,000 against whatever an hour of checkout downtime costs you; a region-level SLA does not protect a single-zone workload; back-off obligations are part of the contract.

7 · Latency, tail and capacity: percentiles, fan-out, queueing, TTFT

Response time is queueing time plus service time, and the two respond to different levers: capacity and code. The tail is what users remember, fan-out multiplies it, queues make it non-linear well before utilisation reaches 100%, and Little’s law turns every second a request is held into capacity you pay for. For model calls the arithmetic has its own names: time to first token, time per output token, and a duration that output length multiplies.

fan-out
1 − 0.99^N of requests meet at least one backend p99: 100 backends → 63%
queueing
R = S / (1 − ρ): at 90% utilisation, ten times the service time
Little’s law
in flight = arrival rate × time held: 20 runs/s × 16.5 s = 330
a model call
duration ≈ TTFT + (output tokens − 1) × ITL: 1.0 + 419 × 0.015 = 7.285 s

Two curves that explain most latency incidents

Percentiles, and how many samples they need

p50 is the typical experience; p90 and p95 describe most users; p99 is one request in a hundred, which a user who makes dozens of requests per session meets every session; p99.9 matters at volume and under fan-out. A percentile is only as good as the samples behind it: at 10 requests per second a 5-minute window holds 3,000 requests, so a 5-minute p99.9 rests on the slowest three. Compute rare percentiles over longer windows or alert on the share above a threshold. In load tests use a constant arrival rate: a generator that waits for each response sends fewer requests exactly when the system is slow and under-reports the tail (coordinated omission).

Mitigations for the tail

  • Timeouts slightly above each dependency’s expected p99, and an overall deadline propagated so later hops know how much time is left; a retry only when the remaining deadline can absorb it.
  • Hedged requests: send a second copy only after the first has been outstanding longer than the p95, so at most about 5% of calls are duplicated; Dean and Barroso report p99.9 for fetching 1,000 values falling from 1,800 ms to 74 ms for 2% more requests. Tied requests cancel the slower copy.
  • For model calls, hedging repeats prefill and a cancelled request has already consumed its input tokens: hedge only short, idempotent calls on the time-to-first-token path, cancel the loser at the winner’s first token, check how the provider bills cancelled streams, and leave the cancelled span’s status Unset.
  • Saturation next to latency on every dashboard: the curve turns upward well before 100%; queue age beats queue depth.

Little’s law in dollars

Twenty runs per second for 16.5 s keeps 330 runs in flight; orchestrator tasks with 1 vCPU and 2 GB handle 40 each, so the peak needs 9 tasks. Cutting the run to 12.9 s (card 3’s four changes) brings that to about 258 runs and 7 tasks, two tasks or about $72 a month at the book’s Fargate prices. Where capacity is expensive the same law bites: a self-hosted model server holds a batch slot for the whole generation, so 120 calls per second of 2.5 s each hold 300 sequences, five GPU replicas at 64 concurrent sequences; calls of 2.0 s (shorter answers) need 240 sequences and four replicas. And the interview version: 50 runs per second taking 8 s each is 400 in flight; cutting 2 s makes it 300, a quarter less memory, connections and batch slots. You can check the in-flight number from telemetry, because the rate of a duration histogram’s _sum equals the mean concurrency.

Measuring latency so the number is true

  1. Measure response time where users feel it and split it into queueing and service time where you can act on it: queueing responds to capacity, service time to code.
  2. Histograms whose buckets bracket the SLO thresholds; merge buckets across instances (keep le); never average percentiles or add them across hops.
  3. Quote percentiles with their sample size: p99.9 over 3,000 requests is three requests; use longer windows or the share-above-threshold.
  4. Budget per hop and propagate a deadline; give each hop a timeout a little above its budget; allow a retry only when the remaining deadline can absorb it: a retried model call alone spends the whole budget.
  5. Put saturation next to latency: CPU, memory working set, active requests, connection pools, queue age, provider rate-limit headroom from response headers.
  6. For LLM paths, track TTFT and tokens per second separately from duration; measure time to first visible token at the client; count retries per dependency against a retry budget; retry only before the first token.
  7. Load-test with a constant arrival rate and read the tail from histograms, not from the generator’s mean.
  8. Read a slow trace by its critical path: root → the child that finished last → repeat; fix the chain, not the longest span off it.

Latency mistakes

  • Averaging percentiles across pods or adding p95s across hops. The fleet p99 depends on how many requests each pod served; the p99 of a sum is not the sum of p99s.
  • A 5-minute p99.9 on a quiet service. Three samples decide it.
  • Hedging model calls like key-value reads. The duplicate repeats prefill and consumes input tokens; hedge short idempotent calls on the TTFT path only, and never after the first token.
  • Marking cancellations as errors. A hedge loser or a user who stopped a stream is not a failure; status Unset, no error.type.
  • Retrying after the first streamed token. The user sees it.
  • Latency from timestamps on two hosts. Clock skew; use each side’s measured durations.
  • Queue depth instead of queue age. Depth looks fine until the oldest message is an hour old.
  • Ignoring saturation until 100%. At 90% utilisation the queue has already multiplied response time by ten.
  • Measuring the first API token as the user’s first token. Hidden reasoning or tool steps come first; the guard that waits for the complete answer adds the whole generation time.

# hedge.py — a second copy after the p95, cancel the loser at the winner's first token, never past the deadline
import asyncio, time

async def hedged_first_token(call, p95_s, deadline_s):
    """call(): async generator of streamed chunks for an idempotent, short model call on the TTFT path."""
    start = time.monotonic()
    first = asyncio.create_task(_first_chunk(call()))
    done, _ = await asyncio.wait({first}, timeout=min(p95_s, deadline_s))
    if done:
        return first.result()                       # the common case: no hedge sent
    remaining = deadline_s - (time.monotonic() - start)
    if remaining <= p95_s:                        # a hedge could not finish in time: wait for the original
        return await asyncio.wait_for(first, remaining)
    second = asyncio.create_task(_first_chunk(call()))
    done, pending = await asyncio.wait({first, second}, timeout=remaining, return_when=asyncio.FIRST_COMPLETED)
    for t in pending:
        t.cancel()                                  # the cancelled span stays Unset: an intended cancellation is not an error
    if not done:
        raise TimeoutError("deadline exceeded before first token")
    return done.pop().result()

async def _first_chunk(stream):
    async for chunk in stream:
        return chunk, stream                        # hand the rest of the stream back to the caller

Six things that make the formula wrong in practice

  1. Provider semantics. Anthropic’s usage.input_tokens counts only tokens after the last cache breakpoint; OpenAI-style usage reports the total with cached as a subset; the conventions use the total. Check, clamp negatives to zero, alert.
  2. Cache-write duration. One-hour writes cost twice the input price, five-minute writes 1.25×; the conventions have one write count, so record the split in custom attributes if you use both.
  3. Discounts and multipliers the span does not show. Batch halves prices; a US-only inference region multiplies by 1.1; fast mode doubles; priority tiers and commitments change the rate. Record batch flag, speed mode, region and route on the span.
  4. Per-use fees. Server-side web search at $10 per 1,000 searches makes one search cost about as much as a mid-size model call; code execution per container-hour. They arrive as usage counts, not tokens.
  5. Reasoning tokens are already inside output; adding them double-counts.
  6. Aggregate spans. invoke_agent may carry run totals; sum inference spans only.

Making cost a trustworthy signal

  1. Version the price table and stamp acme.cost.price_table on every priced span; use a prefix that cannot collide with OpenTelemetry’s namespaces (acme., not app., which the conventions already use).
  2. Compute in all three places, consistently: the application estimates for enforcement (stop the agent at $0.50), the Collector stamps acme.cost.usd and emits unsampled cost metrics, the warehouse recomputes the authoritative figure and reconciles with the invoice.
  3. Only inference spans for token cost; tools, databases, email and egress at their unit prices; every retry counted; shared work by the explicit rules.
  4. Keep sampling out of the numbers: metrics before sampling; th in tracestate on stored traces (the tail-sampling processor writes it behind the usetracestate feature gate); inference spans exported unsampled without content.
  5. Dashboards: cost per run (mean, p95, p99) by agent; spend per hour by model, feature and tenant; cost per successful task; cache-hit ratio (cache-read tokens over input tokens); retry overhead; the count of inference spans with no price.
  6. Alerts are tickets, not pages, unless a runaway loop can spend real money within the hour; the hard stop belongs in the gateway or the agent loop, not in an alert.
  7. Attribute by baggage: tenant and feature propagated as baggage and copied onto spans by a baggage span processor; a tenant label on a cost metric only while tenants number in the hundreds.
  8. Price the telemetry: spans and bytes per run, series per metric, retention per signal; sample traces, keep metrics unsampled, store content by reference.

Cost mistakes

  • The dashboard that fell 90% the day sampling was turned on. It summed cost over stored traces; head sampling at 10% stores a tenth of the spend. Tail sampling distorts the other way: the stored mean was nearly four times the truth.
  • A tenant label on the token histogram. 2,040 series become 1,020,000 and $13 a month becomes $6,630, for one metric.
  • Copying the provider’s input count straight into gen_ai.usage.input_tokens. Uncached input comes out too small, even negative.
  • Summing every span of the trace. The agent span repeats the totals; reasoning is inside output.
  • Hiding idle provisioned capacity in per-request cost. Efficient features look expensive; report the idle remainder as its own line.
  • Cost per run without cost per successful task. A 30% failure rate with retries costs 1.4 times what cost per run suggests.
  • Content in the trace store with long retention. 171 KB of the 188 KB per run is prompts and outputs; it is a privacy review before it is a bill.
  • Paging on cost. A page cannot stop a loop; a run budget, a gateway cap or a quota can.

A judge quality SLO, with its sample size

Judging 2% of 20,000 daily answers yields 400 judgments a day: a 95% interval of about ±2.9 points on a 90% groundedness rate, which supports a daily quality SLO (“at least 88% of judged answers grounded over 7 days”) but not an hourly page. If a model judges “right cause” in the replay set, two engineers grade the first 20 cases too and the judge’s agreement with them is reported before automated scores are trusted.

Time-series foundation models, in one row each

TimesFM 3.0 (Google, Aug 2026; weights non-commercial, 2.5 is Apache-2.0), Chronos-2 (Amazon, Oct 2025, Apache-2.0), Moirai 2.0 (Salesforce, research only), Toto (Datadog, May 2025, Apache-2.0, trained largely on observability metrics, with the BOOM benchmark). Check the licence of the weights, not only the code.

Quality telemetry, and bringing an agent into on-call

  1. Emit every judgment as an event carrying the response id and the span context, so a bad score links to its trace; derive rates in the Collector or backend; sample enough for the window you alert on.
  2. Write the quality SLO and its policy: the judged pass rate over 7 days with a budget; a breach freezes prompt and model changes; judge drift is checked against a human-labelled calibration set each release.
  3. Build the replay set: 20–50 past incidents with postmortems; the alert exactly as it arrived; telemetry only up to that time (time-travel queries with “now” pinned, or recorded fixtures); ground truth with cause category, component, mechanism and acceptable phrasings; postmortems and incident channels removed from anything the agent can retrieve, or you measure search, not investigation.
  4. Measure paired against a baseline on the same incidents (human first notes, or the agent without tools), report the interval, and keep the set as a standing benchmark: every new assistant, model or MCP server runs on it within a month and gets a one-page scorecard.
  5. Guardrails: a Viewer-role service account, --disable-write on MCP servers, no cluster credentials; a separate tenant with low query limits; per-investigation budgets for tool calls, tokens and time; a checker that re-runs citations; telemetry fenced as data; redaction before the model; humans approve rollbacks, restarts and scaling, with an audit log and a kill switch.
  6. Roll out in stages: shadow mode on every page (nothing posted, results compared with postmortems), then opt-in, then default, tracking the metrics throughout.
  7. Give it a catalog: metric names with units and convention versions, a vetted query library, examples written against your Prometheus version and ingestion path, and the rule that empty means unknown.

Where quality telemetry and AI operations go wrong

  • Hourly pages on a judged sample of forty. The interval is wider than the SLO; alert daily, or sample more.
  • Judge drift. A model upgrade changes the judge’s standards and the quality SLI moves with no change in the product; recalibrate against humans each release.
  • Hallucinated evidence accepted because it sounds right. Every claim re-runnable, a checker that re-runs it.
  • Prompt injection through telemetry. Instructions in a user-agent string, an error message or a ticket that the agent later reads. Fence them as data.
  • Leakage in the replay set. The agent finds the postmortem and you measure search.
  • Expensive unscoped queries from an agent taking the platform down during the incident it investigates; low-limit tenant, caps, timeouts.
  • Autonomous changes without approval. Harmful-action rate must be zero; read-only credentials make it so.
  • Vendor accuracy numbers as expectations. 75–82% at a vendor’s customers and 13.8% on ITBench measure different things; the only figure that predicts your results comes from your incidents and your rubric.
  • Scoring every series for anomalies. The multiple-comparisons problem returns with a new logo.

The script: the two-minute answers, the glossary, the worked run, the questions

What to say when asked “how would you make this agent observable?” or “how do you run observability as a platform?”, the thirty terms that come up, the run from card 3 as one worked case, and the questions interviews actually ask, with the shape of a good answer.

Thirty seconds: observability for an AI system

thirty seconds“Everything emits OpenTelemetry with the GenAI conventions: an invoke_agent root with model, tool and retrieval children carrying tokens, latency and results; content opt-in and redacted; RED and cost metrics derived from every span before sampling; judge scores sampled in production as a quality SLO. When something breaks I read the trace before the prompt, and I can tell you what one run costs, where its seconds go, and what observing it costs.”

Two minutes: making an agent observable

two minutes“I start from the questions we will need to answer at 3 a.m.: which step failed, what the model saw, what the tool returned, what it cost, and whether users are worse off. That gives the trace shape: one trace per turn, an invoke_agent root with gen_ai.agent.name and the conversation id, chat spans from the SDK instrumentation with the usage attributes set the conventions’ way (input including cached, cache read and write, output including reasoning), execute_tool spans with result size and typed errors, retrieval spans with index, top-k and returned ids, sub-agents as children, and the tenant and feature from baggage. Content is off by default; where we need it, it goes as events or through the upload hook to a bucket with short retention, redacted in the Collector before export. The Collector has two tiers: node agents add Kubernetes metadata and drop noise; a gateway keyed on trace id normalises names, stamps cost from a versioned price table, derives RED and cost metrics from every span, then tail-samples errors, slow and expensive runs plus a baseline, writing the probability into tracestate. Dashboards show p95 time to first token, tokens per second, error and guardrail rates, cost per run and per successful task; alerts page on SLO burn and ticket on cost. Quality is telemetry too: judge verdicts as evaluation events on a sample, with a weekly quality SLO whose breach freezes prompt and model changes. On Google Cloud that is the Telemetry API into Cloud Trace, Logging and Monitoring with Agent Observability on top; the same spans can go to Langfuse for prompt work, because the backend is an exporter setting.”

Two minutes: running observability as a platform

two minutes“The platform is a production system with its own SLOs: ingestion availability, freshness and correctness by a write-and-read-back canary, query success within a latency bound per tenant, and alert delivery end to end, evaluated from a small meta-monitoring stack in another failure domain, with a watchdog that pages through an external heartbeat when it stops. What breaks first at ten times the load is cardinality, then two writers for one series from Collector-generated metrics, then backpressure nobody alerts on; so there are series budgets per team with a CI lint for identifiers in labels, span metrics computed where each pod has one Collector, and alerts on refused data and remote-write lag well inside the WAL window. Every tier-1 service has SLOs and multiwindow burn-rate alerts; pages without an SLO behind them need a written justification; internal SLOs are stricter than anything we sell. Telemetry has a budget: every team sees its active series, gigabytes and cost, and we prune by usage. We measure the program by median and p90 time to detect and mitigate, alert precision, the share of incidents customers found first, coverage and unit cost. For AI in operations, read-only investigation help that cites the query behind every claim, evaluated on replayed incidents before anyone relies on it; no autonomous changes without approval.”

The billing-support run, start to finish

The agent receives “I was charged twice for my October invoice; please fix it.” A Haiku router classifies it; a Sonnet loop calls search_kb (embedding, vector search), get_billing_history (DynamoDB) and send_email (SES), then writes the answer; a Haiku guard checks it. Fourteen spans. Illustrative, at the book’s list prices of 2 October 2026.

  1. Read the tree. Six chat spans, three execute_tool spans with their CLIENT children, one retrieval, one embeddings, under an invoke_agent under a SERVER span. Every span on the critical path: the loop is sequential.
  2. Cost it. Inference spans only: router $0.001275, step 1 $0.0039, step 2 $0.00908, step 3 $0.0128, final $0.01584, guard $0.00227 = $0.045165; tools and data $0.00011; orchestrator $0.0000057. Run: $0.04528; at a million runs a month, about $45,280. Uncached input is 72% of model spend, cache reads 10.6%, output 17.5%.
  3. Time it. 16.534 s: generation 11.824 s (71.5%), of which the final answer 6.285 s; TTFT 4.100 s (24.8%); tools 0.470 s (2.8%); gaps 0.140 s. The user sees nothing until the guard finishes.
  4. Decide what to fix. The money is in uncached input, so a cache breakpoint at the end of each loop request saves 31.5% for a configuration change (about $14,200 a month). The time is in output, so streaming the final answer with an incremental guard halves the time to first visible token, parallel tool calls remove a step, and a 250-token answer removes 2.5 s. All four: −40% cost, −22% end to end, −47% to first visible token. The vector index and the database are 0.26% of cost and 2.8% of time: leave them alone.
  5. Price the telemetry. About 17 KB of span metadata and 171 KB of content per run with capture on: 188 GB a month, $75.90 at a per-GB price, or $0.0049 per run at a per-span price (10.8% of model spend). So: metrics from every span, traces sampled for errors, expensive runs and a baseline, content by reference with short retention.
  6. Set the SLOs. Availability at the edge; p95 time to first visible token measured at the client; cost per successful task as a ticketed threshold; a weekly groundedness SLO on a 2% judged sample.
  7. Prepare the hostile questions. “Why not just make the vector search faster?” “What does the guard do to latency?” “What happens to this cost at ten times the traffic?” (it scales linearly; the observability bill does not, if content is by reference).

Likely questions, with the shape of a good answer

  • Metrics, logs or traces: which for what? Metrics for bounded aggregates, alerting and long retention; traces for request paths, latency attribution and high-cardinality context; logs for discrete events; profiles for code-level resource use. Exemplars and trace ids link them.
  • What fields does a span have, and which do people get wrong? Card 2: ids, parent, name, kind, times, attributes, events, links, status; ids in names, Error on 4xx server spans, Ok set by libraries, links after creation, attributes dropped at 128, latency across clocks.
  • Which span kind for a model call, a tool, a queue consumer? CLIENT for a remote model, INTERNAL in-process; execute_tool INTERNAL with CLIENT children; PRODUCER and CONSUMER with links for batches; invoke_agent CLIENT for remote, INTERNAL local.
  • How would you compute cost per trace for an agent? Card 8’s formula over inference spans only with a versioned table; tools at unit prices; retries counted; shared work by rules; three places; weighted by adjusted count.
  • Your cost dashboard fell 90% when sampling was turned on. What happened? It summed stored traces under 10% head sampling. Metrics before sampling, or weight by th; tail sampling distorts the other way.
  • A service has a 99.9% SLO over 30 days at 50 requests per second. What is the budget and what pages within an hour? 129.6M requests, 129,600 failures; burn 14.4 on the 1-hour and 5-minute windows: 1.44% errors, 2,592 failures of 180,000, 2% of the budget.
  • Why not page when the error rate exceeds 1% for five minutes? It ignores how much budget is at stake: short spikes page for nothing, slow leaks never page. Burn rate ties paging to budget; two windows give significance and quick recovery.
  • A request fans out to 50 shards with a p99 of 200 ms. What do users see? 39.5% wait on at least one slow shard; the request’s p99 is set by the shards’ p99.98. Reduce fan-out, tighten tails, hedge after the p95, return partial results at a deadline.
  • Fifty runs a second taking 8 s each: how many in flight, and what does cutting 2 s buy? 400, then 300: a quarter less memory, connections and batch slots; verify from the rate of the duration histogram’s sum.
  • Prometheus memory doubled overnight. Walk me through it. Head series and creation rate; the target with scrape_series_added; top label pairs; correlate with deploys; stop the growth (metric_relabel_configs, rollback, sample_limit); memory falls after the next head compaction or a restart; add a review gate.
  • Design tail sampling for 50,000 spans per second. Agents export by trace id to a gateway tier; 2,000 traces per second at 25 spans; 60,000 held at a 30-second wait, about 1.5 GB; span metrics before sampling; keep errors, slow traces, key tenants, a baseline; plan for splitting on scale.
  • Three gateway replicas each compute cost metrics. What goes wrong? Single-writer: routed by trace id they all write the same series and collide in Prometheus and Mimir. Promote a replica label and aggregate it away, route by service, or compute in the node agents.
  • How do you monitor the monitoring? Watchdog through an external dead-man’s switch; a meta stack in another failure domain; alerts on remote-write lag, refused and dropped data, rule-evaluation and notification failures; absence alerts on SLI inputs; a write-and-read-back probe.
  • Why not put the tenant id on a metric? 2,040 series become 1,020,000 for one metric; the tenant goes on spans through baggage and rolls up in the warehouse.
  • What SLOs for the observability platform itself? Ingestion availability, freshness and correctness by canary, query success within a bound per tenant, alert delivery end to end, evaluated from outside.
  • What makes an SLA hollow? Undefined SLI, invisible measurement, open-ended exclusions, discretionary credits, unilateral change rights, a number the architecture cannot support.
  • Where would you use AI during on-call, and where not? Read-only investigation that cites queries, summaries, drafts; never autonomous changes without approval; evaluated on replayed incidents with the hallucinated-evidence rate tracked.
  • How would you evaluate an AI SRE agent? A stratified replay set with telemetry frozen at the page time and leakage removed; top-1 and top-3 accuracy, time to first useful hypothesis, evidence precision, hallucinated evidence, query cost, harmful actions (zero), abstention; paired against a baseline with intervals; shadow then opt-in then default.
  • Explain TTFT, TPOT and end-to-end latency for a model call. Queueing plus prefill; decode time per token; TTFT + (output − 1) × TPOT. Cache reads cut TTFT, shorter answers cut duration, sequential steps multiply everything; measure first visible token at the client.
  • How do you roll out a semantic-convention change across 200 services? Inventory consumers, dual-emit or translate at the Collector, migrate queries, keep both names one retention period, remove and add a CI check.
  • The agent says the dataset does not exist, but it does. Card 3’s six checks, in order; the prompt last.
  • Tell me about a technical direction you set. Problem in numbers, the RFC and the pilot, the objections, the result: pages from 120 to 38 a month at 79% precision, median detection from 14 to 6 minutes.

-- warehouse SQL over exported spans (ClickHouse-style; adapt column names): cost per run, weighted by adjusted count
WITH inference AS (
  SELECT TraceId, SpanAttributes['acme.cost.usd'] AS cost, TraceState FROM spans
  WHERE SpanAttributes['gen_ai.operation.name'] IN ('chat', 'generate_content') AND Timestamp > now() - INTERVAL 1 DAY),
weights AS (
  SELECT TraceId, 1 / (1 - reinterpretAsUInt64(reverse(unhex(rightPad(
           extract(any(TraceState), 'ot=(?:[^,]*;)?th:([0-9a-f]{1,14})'), 14, '0')))) / pow(2, 56)) AS adjusted_count
  FROM inference GROUP BY TraceId)
SELECT sum(cost * adjusted_count) / sum(adjusted_count) AS mean_cost_per_run, sum(cost * adjusted_count) AS total_cost
FROM inference JOIN weights USING TraceId;

-- the six checks as one query: runs without a tool call, tool spans with errors, swallowed errors (OK status, "Error:" result)

       countIf(SpanAttributes['gen_ai.operation.name'] = 'execute_tool') AS tool_calls,
       countIf(SpanAttributes['gen_ai.operation.name'] = 'execute_tool' AND StatusCode = 'Error') AS tool_errors,

FROM spans WHERE Timestamp > now() - INTERVAL 1 DAY GROUP BY TraceId HAVING tool_calls = 0 OR swallowed > 0;

SLOs, budgets, burn rates, availability

SLI = good events ÷ valid events · error budget = 1 − SLO (events or minutes) · budget in time = (1 − SLO) × window
burn rate b = observed error ratio ÷ (1 − SLO) · budget spent in time t = b × t ÷ T · time to exhaustion = T ÷ b
page: b ≥ 14.4 on 1 h and 5 m (2% spent) · b ≥ 6 on 6 h and 30 m (5%) · ticket: b ≥ 1 on 3 d and 6 h (10%) — 30-day window; 28-day: 13.44 / 5.6 / 0.93

Percentiles, fan-out, queues, Little, tokens

response time = queueing time + service time · never average percentiles across instances or add them across hops · samples behind a pN over a window = rate × window × (1 − N)
mean vs tail: 980 × 0.1 + 15 × 1 + 5 × 10 over 1,000 = 0.163 s mean · p50 0.1 s · p99 1 s · p99.9 10 s
fan-out: P(at least one slow backend) = 1 − 0.99^N → N = 5: 4.9% · 10: 9.6% · 20: 18.2% · 50: 39.5% · 100: 63.4% · per-backend percentile needed = 0.99^(1/N)
sequential agent steps: 1 − 0.99^6 = 5.9% of runs contain a slow call, and the delay lands on the user
M/M/1: R = S ÷ (1 − ρ) → ρ 0.5: 2× · 0.7: 3.3× · 0.8: 5× · 0.9: 10× · 0.95: 20×
Little’s law: L = λ × W · rate(duration_sum) = mean in flight · 20 runs/s × 16.5 s = 330 · 50 × 8 = 400 → 50 × 6 = 300
model call: duration ≈ TTFT + (output tokens − 1) × ITL · 1.0 + 419 × 0.015 = 7.285 s ≈ 67 tokens/s · TTFT grows with uncached input, ITL with load and batch size
latency budget: sum of per-hop p95s is conservative (hops rarely all hit p95 together) · 2.0 s TTFT SLO → 1,700 ms allocated, 300 ms headroom
hedging: second copy after the p95 → ≤ 5% duplicates; Dean & Barroso: p99.9 1,800 → 74 ms for +2% requests · for model calls: short idempotent calls, before the first token, cancel the loser
tail-sampling memory ≈ new traces/s × decision_wait × spans/trace × bytes/span · 2,000 × 30 × 25 × 1 KB ≈ 1.5 GB · 5,000 × 30 × 20 × 1 KB ≈ 3 GB per replica
