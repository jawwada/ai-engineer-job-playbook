# 58. Startup and side-business ideas: the ones already in the folder, and twenty more across domains

> **How to read this chapter:** each idea is written the way an investor or a co-founder would want it — the problem, who pays, the smallest product that proves it (buildable with Claude Code and the patterns in Parts 2 and 4 in two to six weeks), the data and the moat, the revenue model, the risks, and the first 90 days. ## 58.1 Ideas already in motion (from the workspace)

### A. RingNest — an AI receptionist and lead-qualification platform for SMBs
**Problem.** Small businesses (home services, legal, clinics, restaurants, multi-location franchises) miss calls, lose leads after hours, and cannot afford staffed answering; existing products (Smith.ai, Goodcall, Slang AI, PolyAI at the enterprise end) leave gaps in verticalization, operations tooling and price. **Product.** An AI receptionist operations console: inbound call handling, lead qualification, appointment booking, knowledge sources with RAG and *knowledge-gap review* (what callers asked that the knowledge base could not answer), QA on calls, integrations and webhooks, usage and billing, team roles, and an API (agents, calls, leads, appointments, knowledge, usage, simulate). The repo already exists as a Next.js/Prisma app with seeded workspaces and API keys, plus a competitor dossier and a build-facing feature master list (table stakes vs competitive vs differentiator vs enterprise). **MVP.** One vertical (home services or dental), telephony via Twilio or LiveKit, a speech-to-speech or chained voice stack (chapter 41), booking into Google/Outlook calendars, SMS confirmations, a daily "gaps and QA" email to the owner. **Moat.** Vertical flows, the knowledge-gap loop (the product gets better from its own calls), integrations, and operational tooling that platform vendors (Retell, Vapi, Bland) do not ship. **Revenue.** Per-location subscription plus per-minute overage; setup fee for custom flows. **Risks.** Voice quality and latency, telephony compliance (recording consent, TCPA for outbound), churn if containment is poor. **90 days.** Ten design partners in one vertical, containment and booking-rate metrics on a dashboard, three integrations, a pricing page.

**Critic's additions: unit economics, the real moat, and the compliance list.** *Unit economics.* A chained voice stack (STT, LLM, TTS, telephony) costs roughly $0.05–0.15 per minute at 2026 list prices, less with caching and a small model for routine turns; a speech-to-speech model costs more per minute but cuts latency. A home-services location receiving 300 calls a month at three minutes each is about 900 minutes, so cost of goods is $50–130 against a price of $250–500 per location per month plus overage above an included allowance; gross margin only works if the knowledge-base setup is self-serve or billed. Price on the missed-call math the owner already knows: one recovered job a month pays for the product. *The moat, honestly.* The knowledge-gap loop is a feature any competitor can copy in a quarter; so are the flows. What compounds is distribution inside one vertical (the dental software reseller, the home-services franchise group), integrations the platforms do not bother with (ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro and Jobber for home services; Dentrix, Open Dental and Curve for dental), and outcome data (which callers booked, which did not, why) that tunes qualification for the whole vertical. The market is crowded — dozens of AI-receptionist startups launched on Vapi and Retell in 2024–2026, and the hosted platforms themselves are moving into verticals — so a generic receptionist is a project, not a company. *Compliance, before the first customer.* Call-recording consent (two-party states such as California require every participant's consent; play the disclosure); disclosure that the caller is talking to an AI (several states now require it in some contexts; verify the ones you sell in); TCPA for any outbound call or text (the FCC treats AI-generated voices as "artificial" voices, so outbound AI calls need prior express consent, and texts need the same); HIPAA with a business-associate agreement the moment a clinic is a customer; PCI scope if the agent ever takes a card number by phone (route to a payment link instead). *Metrics that decide whether to continue:* containment rate, booking rate for callers who asked for an appointment, missed-call recovery versus the owner's old voicemail, and the share of calls the owner marks "would have been better handled by a human".

### B. High-value private-owner car acquisition leads
**Problem.** Dealers and brokers of cars above roughly $300,000 want to buy directly from private owners before the car reaches auctions or other dealers; finding real owners with real intent is manual. **Product.** A daily collection of U.S. listings above the price threshold across marketplaces and club/forum postings, with strict qualification (right vehicle, evidence of private ownership, current sale intent, a stated reason to prioritize such as an observed price reduction or deadline, legitimate contactability, freshness, plausible economics), ranked call lists for an acquisitions team, and a research queue for uncertain items; an operating brief defines what a useful lead is, the calling priorities, and the rules (no inferred financial distress, no invented motivation, contact details or valuations). **MVP.** Already running as a daily automation producing an Excel for the calling team. **Next.** A valuation-and-comparables filter to catch under-priced cars below the threshold; outcome tracking (conversations, offers, purchases) to tune the ranking; the same engine for other scarce assets (boats, machinery, collectibles). **Moat.** Qualification rigor and outcome data; relationships with buyers. **Revenue.** Subscription per acquisitions team, or success fee per purchase. **Risks.** Marketplace terms and anti-scraping measures, privacy law on contact data (collect only what sellers publish for selling, use it only for that purpose), stale listings. See chapter 54.

**Critic's additions: the customer count, the price, and the legal perimeter.** *Customer.* The buyer set is small and reachable: a few hundred exotic and collector-car dealers and brokers in the U.S. who buy above the threshold, plus a long tail of auction consignors. Small markets are fine for a side business and fatal for a venture-scale story; price accordingly. *Pricing anchors.* A subscription of $1,500–3,000 per month per acquisitions team, or a success fee of $2,000–5,000 per purchased car (one purchase in a quarter pays for the year, which is why buyers prefer the success fee and you prefer the subscription; start with a subscription plus a smaller success fee so incentives align). *Moat, honestly.* The listings are public and the qualification rules are copyable; what compounds is outcome data (which leads converted, at what discount to list) and the buyers' trust in a list that never embarrasses them on a call. *Legal perimeter, in detail.* Marketplace terms of use typically prohibit automated collection and commercial reuse; respect robots.txt, rate limits and the terms of each source, prefer official APIs, licensed feeds or the marketplaces' own dealer tools, and treat a terms-of-use breach as a business risk even where no computer-misuse law applies. Under CCPA/CPRA and similar state laws a seller's name and phone number are personal information; handing a ranked contact list to a client can be a "sale" or "sharing", so publish a privacy notice, honor deletion and do-not-sell requests, keep the data for one purpose (contacting a seller about the car they listed) and delete it when the listing is gone. CCPA's size thresholds do not protect a small lead business: a company that earns half or more of its revenue from selling or sharing personal information is covered regardless of size. Selling lists about people you have no direct relationship with can also make you a "data broker": California's Delete Act requires annual registration with the California Privacy Protection Agency and, from August 2026, processing deletion requests from its DROP platform at least every 45 days; Vermont, Texas and Oregon run their own broker registries. Check which apply before the first paid delivery. For outreach, no automated or prerecorded calls and no bulk texting without prior express consent (TCPA and state telemarketing laws); human-dialed calls about the seller's own listing, during reasonable hours, with opt-outs recorded in the system, are the safe pattern. Never infer financial distress, health, family or employment circumstances, and never let the model write a "motivation" the seller did not state; both are already rules in the operating brief and should be tests in the pipeline, not reminders.

### C. Job outreach pipeline and the job-search agent as a product
**Problem.** Applying well is a volume game with a quality constraint. **Product.** The Part 1 agent (tailoring, review-mode submission, tracker, inbox) plus the earlier pipeline (Apify LinkedIn job collection filtered to remote contract roles in the last 24 hours, public-contact extraction, tailored resume and outreach drafts, review packets, SQLite persistence; outbound always human-reviewed). **Productization.** A hosted "career agent" for engineers with a truth file, platform playbooks, and interview prep; or a coaching service that installs the kit for clients. **Moat.** The playbooks and the eval data from real applications. **Revenue.** Subscription, or a per-placement coaching fee. **Risks.** Platform terms (chapter 12), liability for mistakes in applications, commoditization. **90 days.** Fifty users through this book's repo, reply-rate benchmarks by platform, a paid tier with inbox and interview prep.

**Critic's additions: who pays, and the line this product must not cross.** *The competitive set is already large* — Simplify, Teal, Huntr, Jobright, LazyApply, AIApply and the job boards' own tools (several earlier auto-apply startups have already shut down, which says something about the volume model) — and most of it competes on volume ("apply to 500 jobs"), which is exactly what recruiters have learned to filter. The defensible position is the opposite: a truth file that stops the model from inventing experience, review-mode submission so a human approves every application, and interview preparation tied to the specific job. *Who pays.* Individual engineers pay $20–40 a month and churn the day they are hired, so the consumer business is a funnel, not a company. The buyers with budgets are outplacement (employers pay $1,000–3,000 per departing employee to firms that help them land; a modern, engineer-specific outplacement offer is a real B2B wedge), bootcamps and universities (career services licensing per cohort) and coaches who want the kit installed for their clients. *The legal line.* LinkedIn's user agreement prohibits scraping and automation of its site; collectors that extract LinkedIn job posts operate against those terms whatever the tooling vendor says, and accounts get restricted. Build the product on sources that permit it (company career pages, ATS public job feeds such as Greenhouse and Lever boards, aggregator APIs with licenses, email alerts the user signs up for) and keep every outbound message and application behind a human click; an auto-applier that misstates a candidate's experience creates liability for the candidate and for you.

### D. ABJS — account-based job search / consulting outreach with a paid audit sprint
**Problem.** Senior AI engineers get hired through trust, not applications; founders of early-stage AI startups need a credible outside look at measurement, retrieval or agent reliability. **Product.** An inverted account-based-marketing motion: research 15 target companies (seed to Series A, in a chosen niche such as cross-media measurement and AI deduplication for ad-tech, or RAG/agentic systems for vertical SaaS), score fit, and pitch a one-week paid audit sprint (~$3.5K) as the minimal-commitment first engagement; the pipeline loads accounts, researches them, scores "harmony" with the consultant's experience, drafts pitches, and tracks sent/replied/meeting/pilot stages. **Why it works.** The ask is small, specific and useful; the audit produces a report the founder can act on, and often a longer engagement. **Revenue.** Audits at $3–5K, retainers at $10–20K/month, equity-for-advisory occasionally. **Risks.** Niche too narrow or too broad; outreach fatigue; delivering audits that are too generic. **90 days.** Two audits delivered with public (anonymized) write-ups, one retainer.

**Critic's additions: how the fifteen accounts are found, what the message says, and the conversion math.** *Finding accounts.* The buying signals are public: a job post for an "AI engineer, RAG/agents" (they have the problem and no one to own it yet), a seed or Series A announced in the last six months (budget exists; use Crunchbase, Dealroom or Harmonic), a product page claiming "AI-powered" with no evaluation or security content anywhere on the site, a founder posting about hallucinations or costs, and a public incident. Score fit on those signals plus your own domain overlap; fifteen accounts with two signals each beat fifty with none. *The message.* One observation specific to them ("your docs say answers are grounded, but the demo cites a page that does not exist"), one risk in their language (churn, enterprise deal blocked by a security review), one small ask (a one-week audit with a fixed price and a written report). No deck, no "quick call", no flattery. *Conversion math to plan around.* Fifteen well-researched accounts typically yield three to five replies, one or two paid audits and, over two quarters, one retainer; if the first fifteen yield nothing, the niche or the message is wrong, not the volume. *The trap.* Consulting revenue does not compound; design every audit to leave behind a reusable harness, eval set and report template so the tenth audit takes two days and the material becomes a product (chapter 59, idea 14).

### E. Xdemand — AI inventory, demand and pricing optimization for e-commerce and warehouses
**Problem.** Mid-sized online retailers and 3PLs lose margin to stockouts and overstock; 21,000+ U.S. warehouse establishments and an inventory-management software market heading toward $4B by 2028 (figures from the original deck; verify against a current source before quoting them to an investor). **Product.** Demand forecasting (Prophet/NeuralProphet with weather and category effects; 80–90% accuracy in production), price sensing and recommendations, stock-adjusted predictions, out-of-stock-prevention days, warehouse utilization and SKU-warehouse allocation, generative "Ask-AI" product research over reviews and sales, automated reordering, integrations (Amazon, Shopify, WooCommerce, Linnworks, NetSuite, Dynamics). The documented architecture: batch data jobs with a job-status table (Amazon and Linnworks sales, stock status, competitor data, weather), ML jobs (forecasts, stock-adjusted predictions, stockout detection, price sensing), SQL schema, and three repos (website on Azure App Service, pipelines, modeling on Azure ML); a 90-day revenue-maximization formulation (quadratic program over daily prices and quantities with stock and shipment constraints) solved with SciPy. **Status and backlog (from the way-forward deck).** News manager, demand analysis, forecasting, price sensing, price recommendation, product research, Ask-AI live; backlog: weather effects, new-product forecasts via category effects, review impact on demand, competitor price/review scraping, promotion A/B tests, social sentiment; SaaS backend, refined APIs, marketing site, React dashboard, WMS and retail-platform plugins. **Moat.** Integrated decision workflows (not dashboards), forecasting accuracy on messy marketplace data, the agentic copilot. **Revenue.** SaaS by SKU volume plus a share of recovered margin. **Risks.** Data integration effort per customer, forecast trust, incumbents adding AI. **90 days.** Three paying retailers on the SaaS backend, a self-serve Shopify connector, published accuracy benchmarks.

**Critic's additions: the ideal customer, the price, the competitors, and the platform risk.** *Ideal customer.* A brand or marketplace seller with 500–5,000 active SKUs and $5–50M in annual sales, selling on two or more channels (its own Shopify store plus Amazon is the common shape), with one person who "does the ordering" in a spreadsheet; and 3PLs that want to sell planning to their clients. Below 500 SKUs a spreadsheet wins; above 5,000 the buyer already has an ERP planning module and a procurement process you will not survive. *Pricing anchors.* Tiers by SKU count from roughly $300 to $2,000 per month, a 60-day paid pilot priced at the lowest tier, and a margin-share option only after the measurement method is agreed in writing (what counts as an avoided stockout, measured on which SKUs, against which baseline). *Competitors to name in the first meeting* (chapter 61.4): Inventory Planner, Cogsy, Prediko, Flieber, Syrup, Netstock, Lokad; the pitch is not "better forecasts" but decisions with explanations and an agent that answers "why did you recommend 400 units". *Platform risk.* Shopify, Amazon and the ERPs ship native restock suggestions and will keep improving them; the defensible ground is multi-channel data in one model, the pricing-and-stock coupling (the quadratic program), and the copilot over reviews and sales. *Mechanics that need to be true.* "89% accuracy" must be defined (one minus WAPE at SKU-week on the revenue-weighted top of the catalogue; chapter 55), the Ask-AI copilot must run text-to-SQL over a semantic layer with validation rather than raw tables (chapter 23.8), and each new connector costs weeks, so budget for a unified commerce-data API or an open connector framework before promising "integrates with everything".

### F. Systematic trading bots: intraday options and a scalping platform with profit-share billing
**Problem.** Retail and prop-firm traders want disciplined, rule-based execution with risk controls. **Products in the folder.** An intraday options bot for Interactive Brokers (1-minute bars; strategies: opening-range breakout, RSI(2) mean reversion, VWAP momentum, cheap-IV straddles, high-IV-rank strangles, once-a-day defined-risk credit spreads with protective wings; centralized risk gates, forced end-of-day close, email alerts); and ScalpX, a full-stack automated futures scalping platform for TopstepX/ProjectX (FastAPI backend with auth, broker link, bot engine and a 10% profit-share billing on a high-water mark; React dashboard; marketing site; Vercel + Railway deployment). **Why interesting.** The billing model (profit share) aligns incentives and the prop-firm market is large and rules-heavy — a good fit for deterministic risk engines with AI used only for research and monitoring, never for impulsive trading. **Risks.** Regulatory (investment-advice and performance-fee rules differ by jurisdiction and client type), broker API terms, strategy decay, reputational risk from losses; a disclaimer is not a compliance program — get legal advice before charging performance fees. **90 days.** Paper-trading track record with full logs, risk documentation, a compliance review, then a small invited beta.

**Critic's additions: the regulatory map, the business model that stays on the right side of it, and the engineering risks.** *Regulatory map (U.S., in outline; get counsel).* Running strategies on other people's accounts and taking a share of profits looks like investment advice or portfolio management, and performance-based compensation for advisers is restricted to "qualified clients" under the Investment Advisers Act (Rule 205-3; the asset and net-worth thresholds are inflation-adjusted every five years, so check the current figures). Futures and futures options (the ScalpX case) fall under the Commodity Exchange Act: advising on or managing futures trading for others can require registration as a Commodity Trading Advisor with the CFTC and NFA membership unless an exemption applies (for example, the small-adviser exemption with a client-count limit and no public holding-out). Impersonal software and publications that are not tailored to a client have a narrower, publisher-style treatment, which is why "sell the tool, not the trade" matters; a product that places trades for a client under a profit share is not impersonal. Outside the U.S., MiFID II (EU) and the FCA regime (UK) treat automated portfolio management as a licensed activity; Germany's BaFin takes the same view. *The model that stays clean.* Charge a flat subscription for software that runs under the user's own broker credentials on the user's own account, with the user configuring and starting each strategy; publish research and backtests with full methodology and no return promises; do not custody funds, do not pool accounts, and do not take performance fees until a lawyer has written down why you may. Profit-share billing against a prop-firm evaluation account has an extra wrinkle: the "profits" are simulated until the firm pays out, so bill on paid-out profits only, and read the prop firm's rules on automation (several restrict unattended bots, copy trading and high-frequency execution, and ban "exploiting" simulated fills). *Engineering risks.* Strategy decay and overfitting (walk-forward tests, out-of-sample periods, parameter stability), execution assumptions (slippage and fill models that are too kind), broker and prop-firm API limits, and a risk engine that must be deterministic: hard daily loss limit, position caps, a kill switch independent of the strategy process, forced flat before the close, and an audit log of every order. AI belongs in research, monitoring and post-mortems, not in the order path.

## 58.3 How to pick one (a scoring sheet)

Score each idea 1–5 on: *pain intensity* (do customers already pay to solve it badly?), *access* (can you reach ten buyers this month?), *data advantage* (do you get proprietary outcome data from day one?), *unfair skill* (does your background make you credible in a week?), *buildability* (can Claude Code plus the patterns here produce a demo in two weeks?), *regulatory drag*, *willingness to pay*. Anything under 25 of 35 is a project, not a company. Then do the ABJS motion (idea D) with the top idea: fifteen target accounts, a paid pilot ask, and the evidence from the pilot decides whether to build the product.
