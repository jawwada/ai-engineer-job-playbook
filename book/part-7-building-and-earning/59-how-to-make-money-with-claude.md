# 59. How to make money with Claude: income playbooks for AI engineers

> **The idea:** the same skills that get you hired can be sold directly — faster, and with more control — if you package them. This chapter lists the proven ways engineers earn with Claude (and the Claude Agent SDK, Claude Code, MCP and the patterns in this book), with pricing, the first-customer path, what to build in week one, and the risks. Pick two, not ten.

## 59.1 Consulting and FDE-as-a-service

1. **Paid audit sprint.** One week, fixed price (\$3–5K), one deliverable: an audit of a company's RAG/agent/measurement system with an eval set, traces and a prioritized fix list. Sell it with the ABJS motion (chapter 58 D): fifteen researched accounts, a problem-first message, a small ask. Week one: build the audit template (eval harness + report skeleton) with Claude Code so every audit reuses 80%.
2. **Implementation retainers.** After an audit: \$10–25K/month for 2–3 days a week to build the system; use the kit of skills and reference architectures in this repo to deliver fast; bill on milestones with acceptance criteria (chapter 35).
3. **Fractional Head of AI / AI architect** for Series A–C companies: \$8–15K/month for strategy, vendor selection, hiring and reviews; Claude drafts the documents, you own the judgement.
4. **Cloud-partner delivery.** Register as a partner or subcontractor for Google Cloud, AWS or Azure consultancies that need agent engineers for customer projects; day rates \$900–1,600 in the US for senior AI work (anchors, not quotes; verify against current rate surveys); the cloud labs in chapter 34 are your credentials. *Mechanism:* the consultancies that win cloud-funded projects are in the partner programs (AWS Partner Network, Google Cloud Partner Advantage, the Microsoft AI Cloud Partner Program) and staff from benches of certified people, so the entry ticket is one professional certification per cloud you claim (for example the AWS Machine Learning or AI Practitioner tracks, Google's Professional Machine Learning Engineer, Microsoft's Azure AI Engineer Associate), a profile on the consultancy's bench list, and one public lab that shows the runtime they sell.
5. **Contract roles through Dice and LinkedIn** (Part 1 of this book): corp-to-corp or W2 contracts at \$90–140/h for senior AI engineering; the job agent fills the pipeline while you deliver. *Mechanism:* corp-to-corp requires your own entity (an LLC or S-corp with its own insurance and invoicing), W2 contracts run through the staffing firm's payroll at a lower rate with fewer obligations; work authorization decides which is open to you, and employer-tied visas generally allow income only from the sponsoring employer, so confirm your status with an immigration adviser before signing anything.

## 59.2 Productized services (recurring, semi-automated)

6. **Lead-generation service** for one niche (chapter 54, recipes 2–3): weekly ranked lists with evidence and drafted outreach; \$1–3K/month per client; built on official APIs, licensed data and Apify collectors with the legal rules followed. *The legal rules, spelled out:* commercial email in the U.S. falls under CAN-SPAM (accurate headers, a physical postal address, a working opt-out honored within ten business days, no harvested addresses); prospects in the EU and UK fall under GDPR and the e-privacy rules (B2B outreach on a legitimate-interest basis needs a privacy notice, a record of the source, and immediate opt-out; national rules differ, and Germany's unfair-competition law requires prior consent even for most B2B cold email), Canada's CASL is consent-based and strict; a service that sells lists about people it has no direct relationship with may also be a data broker that must register (California's Delete Act and its DROP deletion platform, plus registries in Vermont, Texas and Oregon; chapter 58 B); phone and SMS outreach falls under the TCPA and state telemarketing laws (no autodialed or prerecorded calls or texts without prior express consent, honor the national and state do-not-call lists). Every collector must respect the source's terms of use and robots rules, and the client, not you, sends the outreach, from their domain, with their opt-out mechanism — you supply the list and the evidence.
7. **Agent operations service**: you run and improve a client's support/ops agent — evals, prompt and tool changes, cost reviews — for a monthly fee tied to containment or cost per ticket.
8. **Document-processing pipelines** for SMB finance/legal: invoices, contracts, claims → structured data and trackers; per-document pricing; human review included.
9. **Content and compliance review** for regulated marketers (chapter 58, idea 1) delivered as a service before it is a product.
10. **Interview and career coaching for engineers** using this repo: install the kit, run mock interviews with `/interview-prep`, review resumes; \$150–300/hour or packages; the book is your funnel.

## 59.3 Products

11. **Micro-SaaS from a recipe**: the motivated-seller finder for one asset class, the AI receptionist for one vertical (RingNest), the inventory copilot for Shopify (Xdemand lite), a FinOps dashboard for LLM spend. Build the MVP in two to six weeks with Claude Code; charge from day one; ten design partners before features.
12. **Claude Code plugins, skills and MCP servers**: packaged workflows for a niche (e.g., a "FDE delivery kit", a "RAG eval kit", an MCP server for a vertical SaaS API) — sold as licenses or as the free top of a consulting funnel; marketplaces and registries are forming. *Where they live in 2026:* Claude Code plugin marketplaces (any Git repository can host one, so a consultancy can publish its own), the public MCP Registry and Docker's MCP Catalog for servers, and the enterprise-internal registries the clouds added (AWS's Agent Registry in AgentCore). *How the money works:* almost nobody pays for a plugin directly; the plugin is the demo that earns the audit, the MCP server is the integration a SaaS vendor pays you to build and maintain for their customers, and the "kit" is what makes your retainer cheaper to deliver than a competitor's. License it under a permissive license if it is the funnel, under a commercial license if it is the product, and never ship client-specific prompts or data inside it.
13. **Vertical agents on marketplaces**: Google Cloud Marketplace, AWS Marketplace, Salesforce AppExchange, Microsoft Marketplace (which absorbed Azure Marketplace and AppSource in 2025); the clouds co-sell with partners who bring agents for their customers. *Mechanism:* a marketplace listing lets the customer pay with committed cloud spend they have already budgeted, which removes a procurement step; the listing requires a partner registration, a security review and metered billing integration, which takes weeks, so start it before the product is finished.
14. **Datasets and eval suites**: curated, licensed evaluation sets and red-team suites for a domain (legal, insurance, support) sold to teams shipping agents; chapter 53's harness is the product.

## 59.5 Trading, markets and automation income (with the warnings)

19. **Systematic trading tools** (chapter 58 F): sell the software and the research, not advice; profit-share billing and performance fees have regulatory requirements that differ by jurisdiction and client type — get legal advice before charging them; never promise returns. The regulatory map in chapter 58 F (adviser performance-fee rules, commodity-trading-advisor registration for futures, MiFID II and FCA authorization in Europe, prop-firm automation rules) applies in full; the clean model is a flat subscription for software that runs on the user's own account under the user's own credentials, with no custody, no pooling and no performance fee until counsel has signed off.
20. **Automation for your own businesses**: the quietest way to earn with Claude is to run a small business (an agency, an e-commerce store, a rental portfolio) with agents doing the operations work; income comes from the business, Claude cuts the cost.

## 59.6 The operating rules

- **Sell outcomes, price on value, deliver with evals.** Every engagement has an acceptance test; every product has a metric the customer cares about.
- **Own the data loop.** Keep the outcome data (what converted, what was accurate) — it is the moat for products and the proof for services.
- **Stay legal.** Platform terms (scraping, automation), privacy law (GDPR/CCPA), outreach law (CAN-SPAM, TCPA), financial regulation (advice, performance fees), employment law (contractor status, visa constraints on side income — check yours). Three items engineers skip and regret: *your current employment contract* (invention-assignment and moonlighting clauses can give your employer ownership of side projects built in your field — read them, and get written consent where they apply); *contractor-status rules* (IR35 in the UK, AB5-style tests in California, "scheinselbständigkeit" in Germany) that decide whether a long single-client engagement is really employment; and *the model provider's usage policies*, which bind what your product may do and require disclosure in some contexts — read them before you promise a feature to a client.
- **Contract before code.** Every paid engagement has a short master agreement and a statement of work with the deliverable, the acceptance test, the price and payment terms, an IP clause (you keep your kit, they own the deliverable), a liability cap, a confidentiality clause, and a data-processing agreement when you touch personal data. Carry professional-liability (errors and omissions) insurance before the first retainer; some clients will require it, and the ones that do not are the ones you want it for.
- **Automate your own delivery first.** Templates, skills, harnesses and checklists so a one-week audit takes two days of your time.
- **Pipeline discipline.** Fifteen targeted conversations a week beat a hundred broadcasts; the Part 1 agent and the ABJS pipeline keep it moving while you deliver.
- **Reputation compounding.** Publish the anonymized results of every engagement (with permission): numbers, architecture, lessons. The repo and the write-ups are the marketing.

**The arithmetic, and what goes wrong in year one.** *Arithmetic first.* A solo consultant rarely bills more than 120 days a year once selling, admin and unpaid scoping are counted; at \$1,200 a day that is \$144K of revenue before tax, insurance, tools and the unpaid weeks between clients. A retainer of \$12K a month for two or three days a week is worth more than the day rate suggests because it removes the selling gap; two retainers plus one audit a month is a sustainable year, three retainers is a burnout. Price audits on the value of the decision they inform (a \$4K audit that stops a \$200K build is cheap) and retainers on outcomes with a floor, never on hours alone. *What goes wrong.* Pricing too low and then resenting the client; scope creep with no change-order clause; data access that arrives in week four of a six-week engagement (put a data-readiness gate and a start-date clause in the SOW); one client that is 80% of income; demos that work on the sample data and fail on theirs (ask for a real sample before quoting); unbilled "quick questions" that become a free support line; forgetting that a retainer client expects responsiveness, so plan at most two at a time. *The Claude-specific advantage, stated plainly:* the kit (skills, harnesses, templates, reference architectures) is what lets one person deliver what used to need three, and that leverage is the margin — spend the first month building the kit, not the website.

**One-paragraph plan for the next 90 days.** Pick one niche where your resume is already credible; build the audit template and one lab in that niche; run the ABJS outreach to fifteen accounts; deliver two audits; write them up; convert one into a retainer; meanwhile let the job agent keep applying so the floor is covered. Decide at day 90 whether the product (a recipe from chapter 54 or an idea from chapter 58) deserves the next quarter.
