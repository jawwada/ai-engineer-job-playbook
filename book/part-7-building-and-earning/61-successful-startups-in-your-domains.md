# 61. Successful startups in the domains on the resume: what they do and how they do it

> **Why this chapter:** the fastest way to sound like an insider in an interview — or to pick a startup idea — is to know who already wins in your domains and what mechanism makes them work. The domains below are the ones on the reference resume in Part 5: ad-tech and marketing measurement, marketing content and compliance, creative AI for film and sound, retail inventory and pricing, energy and utilities, insurance and telematics, telecom analytics, conversational and voice AI, and trading/prediction markets. Company facts are as of October 2026 and change fast; verify funding and status before quoting them. (Acquisitions, renames and shutdowns named in this edition were re-checked on 2 October 2026; where a status could not be confirmed, the text says "verify".)

## 61.1 Ad-tech, measurement and marketing analytics

- **AppLovin (Axon)** — the public-company proof that ML-driven ad ranking and bidding at scale prints money: a two-tower-style retrieval plus deep ranking over enormous event streams, continuous retraining, and tight A/B discipline. *How:* own the auction, own the data, optimize for advertiser outcomes, not clicks. (AppLovin publishes little about Axon's internals; the architecture described is the industry-standard one, not a disclosed design, so say "likely" in an interview. The company sold its own games business in 2025 to become a pure ad platform, and an SEC inquiry into its data-collection practices was reported in late 2025; check its status before citing AppLovin as a model.)
- **Moloco** — "machine-learning-as-the-product" for performance advertising and retail media: deep ranking and bidding models sold to app publishers and retailers. *How:* models trained on the customer's first-party data with privacy-safe pipelines; outcome-based pricing.
- **The Trade Desk (Kokai)** — AI in the DSP: forecasting, bidding, audience modeling; relevant as the buyer-side counterpart to broadcaster ad serving. *How:* platform scale plus explainable optimization for traders.
- **Measurement and MMM startups — Haus, Recast, Measured, Lifesight, Adelaide (attention metrics), Rockerbox (acquired by DoubleVerify)** — the post-cookie answer to attribution: Bayesian marketing-mix models, geo-experiments and incrementality testing as products. *How:* experiment design plus Bayesian inference and clear dashboards; sold to marketing and finance together. *Opportunity:* cross-media measurement and AI deduplication for broadcasters (chapter 58, idea 2, sold through the audit sprint of idea D).
- **Retail media networks (Criteo, Instacart Ads, Amazon Ads tooling startups)** — ad serving and measurement inside commerce; same ranking/pricing/forecasting problems as broadcaster ad-tech. *How:* the retailer owns closed-loop purchase data, so the network can rank ads on predicted conversion rather than clicks, price inventory from measured incrementality, and sell "sales lift" reports that open-web ad-tech cannot produce.

**Critic's additions: more names in measurement, cross-media and the clean-room layer.** The broadcaster-side problem on the resume (cross-media reach and frequency, deduplicating audiences across linear, streaming and digital) has its own competitive set, and it is the one to name in an interview. *Cross-platform TV measurement:* VideoAmp, iSpot.tv (which acquired 605), Comscore, Samba TV, Nielsen ONE (the incumbent currency) and EDO (outcome measurement for TV). *How they do it:* automatic content recognition from smart TVs and set-top-box logs give household-level exposure, a calibrated panel corrects for coverage bias, identity graphs match exposure to outcomes, and deduplication across screens is a probabilistic match with a privacy budget rather than a deterministic join; the currency fight is about whose calibration advertisers trust. *Clean rooms, where the dedup now happens:* LiveRamp (with Habu), InfoSum (acquired by WPP in 2025), AWS Clean Rooms, Google's Ads Data Hub, Snowflake Data Clean Rooms — two parties compute overlap and reach without either seeing the other's rows, which is exactly the setting for "AI deduplication" products. *Attribution and MMM for e-commerce brands:* Northbeam, Triple Whale, Measured, Prescient AI, Mutinex (Australia's MMM startup). *Buy-side and sell-side with heavy ML:* StackAdapt, Zeta Global, Quantcast, Scibids (acquired by DoubleVerify), Rokt (e-commerce transaction ads; acquired mParticle in 2025) and Innovid (acquired by Mediaocean in 2025). The gap worth probing: mid-sized broadcasters and streamers that cannot afford VideoAmp-scale contracts but need an auditable dedup and incrementality method — the ABJS audit sprint in chapter 58 D starts there.

## 61.2 Marketing content, review and compliance

- **Jasper, Writer, Typeface, Copy.ai** — enterprise content generation with brand voice and governance; Writer builds its own models and a knowledge graph for enterprise facts; Typeface specializes in brand-safe multimodal content. *How:* brand rules + retrieval + review workflows + integrations with the marketing stack.
- **Adobe GenStudio and Firefly Services** — the incumbent's answer: generation plus brand compliance inside the creative suite; Workfront for process (the system of record in chapter 36.1).
- **Compliance-review products — Red Oak, Hearsay (Yext), Saifr (Fidelity Labs' marketing-compliance AI), PerformLine (marketing compliance monitoring), Smart Communications (SmartComms; customer-communications management with compliance controls, adjacent rather than a review tool), Pencil's brand checks on generated ads, Writer's governance** — automated review of marketing communications for financial services. *How:* rules engines plus LLM classification with citations and audit trails; the marketing-compliance copilot (chapter 58, idea 1) competes here.

**Critic's additions: the rules that make this market, and more names.** *The rule is the product spec.* In the U.S., FINRA Rule 2210 governs communications with the public (retail communications need principal pre-approval, certain pieces are filed with FINRA, performance claims and comparisons have specific requirements) and the SEC's Marketing Rule for investment advisers governs testimonials, performance presentations and substantiation; the EU and UK have equivalents in MiFID II's fair-clear-not-misleading standard and the FCA's financial-promotions regime, and pharma has FDA promotional-labeling rules with medical-legal-regulatory review. Products in this space win by encoding those rules as checkable items with citations and by producing the audit record the regulator asks for; a copilot that only "reviews for tone" sells nothing. *More names.* Marketing-operations and content-governance platforms with review workflows where financial-services marketers already live: Aprimo (marketing resource management with compliance workflow), Seismic and Highspot (sales-content governance), Markup AI (formerly Acrolinx, renamed in 2025; automated content-standards checking), Persado (AI-generated marketing language with approval trails, sold into banks), Frontify and Bynder (brand guidelines and digital asset management with brand-check features). Supervision and archiving, which any review product must integrate with: Smarsh, Global Relay, Proofpoint. *How the winners work:* a pre-approval workflow where the AI pre-reads an asset and attaches findings with rule ids, a disclosure library that inserts the required statements per product and jurisdiction, versioned approvals with reviewer identity and timestamps, and an archive export regulators can read; the model is the fast first reviewer, never the signature.

## 61.3 Creative AI: film, video, sound

- **Runway, Pika, Luma, Kling (Kuaishou), Black Forest Labs (FLUX), Midjourney, Stability AI** — generative video and image models and tools; Runway sells to studios with workflows (Gen-4, control tools) and a film fund. *How:* model quality plus editing controls plus rights-conscious licensing.
- **ElevenLabs, Cartesia, Suno, Udio, Stable Audio** — voice and music generation; ElevenLabs expanded from TTS into dubbing, conversational agents and a marketplace. *How:* best-in-class models, APIs, and vertical products (dubbing, audiobooks, agents).
- **Descript, Captions (renamed Mirage in 2025 as it moved into its own video models), Opus Clip** — AI-native editing for creators; **Flawless, Wonder Dynamics (acquired by Autodesk, 2024), Metaphysic (acquired by DNEG's Brahma, 2025)** — VFX-grade generative tools (lip-sync, CG characters, de-aging) adopted by studios with human-in-the-loop pipelines. *How:* integrate into existing post-production workflows; solve a specific expensive step.
- **MMAudio and video-to-audio research (academic)** — the foley problem; a product gap for sound-design teams (resume use case 36.2). The gap narrowed in 2025 when Google's Veo 3 and OpenAI's Sora 2 began generating synchronized audio with generated video; it remains open for the professional case — adding controllable, editable, stem-separated sound to *real* footage, with the rights to every sample documented.

**Critic's additions: rights are the mechanism, and more names.** In film and music the model is rarely what decides the sale; the chain of title is. Studios buy tools whose training data, outputs and performer consents they can defend in a contract, and the market has reorganized around that since 2024.

- *Licensing as strategy.* Disney invested $1 billion in OpenAI in December 2025 and licensed more than 200 characters for Sora, the clearest sign that the large rights holders want to be paid partners, not plaintiffs; Lionsgate's 2024 deal with Runway (a model fine-tuned on the studio's own library) is the template for a private, studio-owned model. In music, Udio settled with Universal and then Warner in late 2025 and pivoted to licensed creation, and Suno settled with Warner and signed a licensing deal; check the status of the remaining label suits before citing them.
- *Litigation as the risk.* Disney and Universal sued Midjourney in June 2025 and Warner Bros. Discovery followed in September 2025; Getty's UK case against Stability AI produced a largely unfavorable judgment for Getty in late 2025 (appeals and the U.S. case continue; verify). Any product that generates from a model trained on unlicensed material inherits this exposure, which is why enterprise buyers ask for indemnities.
- *Labor as a requirement.* The 2023 SAG-AFTRA film and TV contract requires informed consent and compensation for digital replicas, and the 2024–2025 video-game strike ended in July 2025 with similar AI protections; a voice or likeness tool that cannot record consent per performer, per use, cannot be sold to a union production.
- *Provenance as a feature.* C2PA content credentials attached at generation time, and an audit log of prompts, seeds, source assets and model versions per shot, are what a studio's legal team asks for.

More names to know, with the mechanism each represents: **Google Veo and Flow** (frontier video generation with native audio inside a filmmaking tool); **OpenAI Sora** (the licensed-characters model above); **Adobe Firefly** (trained on licensed Adobe Stock and public-domain content and sold with enterprise indemnity, the "commercially safe" position); **Moonvalley** (its Marey model is trained on licensed footage and sold to studios as a clean alternative); **Synthesia** and **HeyGen** (avatar video for corporate communication, built on consented, recorded presenters); **Lightricks LTX** (an open-weight video model studios can run privately); **Topaz Labs** (upscaling and restoration, a narrow, expensive post-production step automated); **AudioShake** (stem separation licensed by labels and studios, the enabling step for remixing and dubbing); **Deepdub** and **Respeecher** (dubbing and consented voice cloning for film and TV; Respeecher's voice work for Lucasfilm's young Luke Skywalker is the familiar example). The gap for an engineer with the resume's creative-platform background: search, tagging and rights metadata across a studio's archive (chapter 58, idea 3), which none of the generation companies want to build.

## 61.4 Retail operations: inventory, pricing, forecasting

- **Syrup Tech, Flieber, Inventory Planner (Sage), Cogsy, Prediko, Toolio** — demand forecasting and inventory planning for brands and marketplace sellers; Syrup raised on "AI-native planning"; Flieber targets multi-channel sellers. *How:* connect to Shopify/Amazon/ERPs, forecast at SKU-channel grain, recommend orders, measure stockouts and overstock. Xdemand (chapter 58, idea E) sits here.
- **Lily AI** — product-attribute enrichment from images and text for retailers (multimodal extraction as a product).
- **Crisp** — retail data platform for CPG brands (retailer data ingestion and analytics).
- **Pricing: Competera, Prisync, Intelligence Node (acquired by Interpublic in 2024, part of Omnicom since the 2025 merger), Revionics (part of Aptos), Antuit.ai (part of Zebra Technologies since 2021), Zilliant** — price optimization with elasticity models. *How:* competitor scraping (with the legal care in chapter 54), elasticity estimation, rules and guardrails for category managers.
- **Blue Yonder, o9, Kinaxis, RELEX** — the enterprise planning incumbents adding agents; the bar for integration depth.

**Critic's additions: the forecasting and pricing mechanics, and more names.** *Forecast distributions, not numbers.* The winners in inventory planning forecast quantiles, not points, because the order decision depends on the spread: for a single period the optimal stock level is the demand quantile at the critical ratio, underage cost divided by underage plus overage cost (a product with a 60% margin and cheap storage should be stocked at a high quantile; a perishable low-margin one at a low quantile). *Unconstrain the history first.* A stockout hides demand, so sales on out-of-stock days understate it; models trained on raw sales under-forecast exactly the items that sell out, which is the most common mistake in marketplace forecasting and the first thing to check in an audit. *Encode the real constraints:* supplier lead time and its variance, minimum order quantities, case packs, container fill, cash budget and warehouse capacity, which turn "how many" into a small optimization problem rather than a forecast. *Pricing:* elasticity is estimated from price experiments where possible and from natural price variation with controls for promotions, seasonality and competitor prices otherwise; cross-price effects (cannibalization within a category) matter more than own-price effects for assortment decisions; and every recommendation passes guardrails (minimum margin, price-ladder consistency, manufacturer advertised-price policies, maximum change per week). A legal note for pricing products: tools that pool competitors' non-public pricing data to recommend prices draw antitrust scrutiny (the U.S. Department of Justice's 2024 case against RealPage is the reference); use public prices and the customer's own data only.

More names to know: **Lokad** (probabilistic forecasting and decision optimization, the clearest public writing on the quantile approach); **Netstock** (inventory optimization for small and mid-sized companies on ERPs such as Business Central and NetSuite); **Cin7** and **Linnworks** (inventory and order management, the systems a planning tool must plug into); **PROS** (B2B and airline pricing, taken private by Thoma Bravo in 2025 for about $1.4 billion); **Pricefx** and **Vendavo** (B2B price management); **Omnia Retail** and **Quicklizard** (dynamic pricing for retailers); and the platform risk itself — **Shopify** and **Amazon** keep adding native forecasting, restock and pricing assistants, so an independent tool must win on multi-channel data and on explaining its recommendations.

## 61.5 Energy and utilities

- **Kraken Technologies (Octopus Energy's platform, being spun out)** — the utility operating platform licensed to other utilities; the model for software-led energy companies. *How:* one platform for billing, grid flexibility and customer service with ML throughout. Octopus sold a minority stake in Kraken in December 2025 at an $8.65 billion valuation as the first step of a full demerger (check whether the demerger has completed); the platform reported about 75 million customer accounts across 27 countries in late 2025, with licensees such as E.ON, EDF, Origin Energy, Plenitude and the water utility Severn Trent.
- **Amperon, Gridmatic, Myst AI (acquired by Snowflake in 2023), Kevala, Camus Energy, Utilidata** — forecasting (load, price, renewables), grid analytics and edge intelligence. *How:* domain-tuned time-series models with weather, operations integration, and credibility with regulated buyers. District-heating optimization (36.5) is the same discipline.
- **Enode, Texture** — energy device APIs (EVs, batteries, heat pumps) that enable demand-response products; **Arcadia** for utility data.
- **Inspection and vegetation management: Overstory, AiDash, Buzz Solutions, Sharper Shape** — satellite/drone imagery plus ML for utility asset risk (36.5's drone-inspection work).

**Critic's additions: how district-heating optimization makes money, how Kraken locks in, and more names.** *District heating, mechanically.* The operator forecasts heat demand per zone from weather (outdoor temperature, wind, solar radiation), calendar and building mix; it then chooses the lowest supply temperature that still meets the minimum at the critical customer at the end of the network, because every degree lower cuts distribution losses and raises heat-pump efficiency; finally it dispatches production (combined heat and power, heat pumps, electric and gas boilers, waste heat, thermal storage) against electricity and fuel prices — a mixed-integer optimization with the forecast as input. A hydraulic digital twin of the network checks that pressures and flows stay within limits before a set-point goes live. The value is measurable in fuel, losses and peak capacity avoided, which is why buyers accept savings-based pricing; the obstacles are 12–18-month municipal procurement, operators who want an engineer on the phone, and data locked in SCADA systems. *Kraken's lock-in, mechanically.* A utility that migrates millions of customer accounts, meter data and tariffs onto Kraken does not migrate them again; licensing is priced per account, so revenue grows with every licensee's customer base, and each new market adds tariff and regulatory logic that later licensees reuse.

More names to know: **Gradyent** (digital twins and optimization for district heating and cooling networks; raised a EUR 28 million Series B in 2025 — the closest company to the resume's district-heating work); **Leanheat** (building-level heat optimization, part of Danfoss); **Kamstrup** (smart heat and water meters, the data source most district-heating optimization depends on); **Uplight** (utility customer engagement and flexibility, which acquired the DERMS provider AutoGrid in 2024); **Bidgely** (AI disaggregation of smart-meter data for utilities); **Oracle Opower** (behavioral energy reports, the original utility-analytics business); **Voltus** (aggregating distributed resources into wholesale-market capacity); **Tomorrow.io** (commercial weather forecasting, an input to every load and renewables model). The interview point: in regulated energy the buyer pays for credibility with the regulator and the control room, so the winners publish their forecast accuracy, run in shadow mode for a season before controlling anything, and keep a human operator in the loop for set-point changes.

## 61.6 Insurance and telematics

- **Lemonade** — AI-native insurer (chat-first underwriting and claims). **Shift Technology, Tractable, CCC, Snapsheet** — claims automation (fraud detection, photo-based damage estimation). *How:* computer vision for damage, graph analytics for fraud, straight-through processing with adjuster review.
- **Sixfold, Gradient AI, Federato, Planck (acquired by Applied Systems in 2024)** — underwriting intelligence and submission triage with LLMs and external data. *How:* document understanding plus risk models plus workflow for underwriters.
- **Cambridge Mobile Telematics, Zendrive (its technology acquired by Intuit for Credit Karma in 2024), Octo, Arity (Allstate)** — smartphone telematics for insurers (pay-how-you-drive; 36.6). *How:* sensor-fusion models for driving behaviour, distraction detection, crash detection; data partnerships with insurers.

**Critic's additions: how telematics and claims AI actually reach a price or a payment, the rules, and more names.** *Telematics to premium.* Trip data is map-matched to the road network (the resume's PhD topic) so that speed can be scored relative to the posted limit, then summarized into rating features per 100 miles — hard braking, rapid acceleration, phone handling while moving, night driving, mileage. Those features enter the insurer's rating plan (a GLM or a constrained gradient-boosted model) as filed rating factors; a model that cannot be explained factor by factor to a state regulator does not ship. State rules differ — California, for example, limits usage-based auto pricing essentially to verified mileage (verify the current rule) — and the NAIC's 2023 model bulletin on insurers' use of AI, adopted by many states, and Colorado's unfair-discrimination law for insurance algorithms require governance, testing for unfair bias and vendor oversight. In the EU, AI used for risk assessment and pricing in life and health insurance is high-risk under the AI Act. *Claims, mechanically.* Photo-based estimation is trained on images paired with final repair invoices, so accuracy is measured against what the repair actually cost, not against an adjuster's guess; low-severity claims that pass fraud and coverage checks go straight through to payment, everything else goes to an adjuster with the model's estimate attached. Fraud detection works on graphs (shared phone numbers, addresses, repair shops, doctors across claims), which is why a vendor that sees many insurers' claims has a data advantage no single insurer can match.

More names to know: **Root** (a telematics-first auto insurer, the test of whether driving data beats traditional rating at scale); **Next Insurance** (small-business insurance sold online, acquired by ERGO, part of Munich Re, in 2025); **Coalition** and **At-Bay** (cyber insurance that scans a customer's external attack surface and prices and coaches on it); **CAPE Analytics** (property attributes from aerial imagery, acquired by Moody's in 2025); **ZestyAI** (climate and property-risk models approved for rating by regulators); **Akur8** (transparent machine-learning pricing that produces regulator-friendly rating tables); **hyperexponential** (a pricing platform for commercial and specialty underwriters); **EvolutionIQ** (claims guidance for disability and injury claims, acquired by CCC for $730 million in 2025); **Netradyne**, **Samsara** and **Motive** (AI dash cams and fleet telematics, the commercial-fleet counterpart to smartphone scoring). The opening for the resume's background: driver-behaviour scoring and coaching for small fleets and their insurers, built on the camera vendors' APIs rather than against them (chapter 58, idea 7).

## 61.7 Telecom and location analytics

- **Rakuten Symphony, Totogi (TelcoDR), Mavenir, Subex, Netcracker's AI** — AI for telco operations, BSS/OSS and revenue assurance; **Aira, Cellwize (Qualcomm)** for RAN optimization. *How:* network data at scale, closed-loop automation, agents for care and ops.
- **Placer.ai, SafeGraph, Near (filed for bankruptcy in December 2023, months after going public), Unacast (merged with Gravy Analytics in 2023), Pass_by, Blis (acquired by T-Mobile in 2025)** — footfall and location analytics for retail site selection and marketing (the Telefónica-era work on the resume). *How:* privacy-safe aggregation of location signals, benchmarks and dashboards.

**Critic's additions: how operator data becomes footfall, why location data is a legal minefield, and more names.** *Operator data, mechanically.* A mobile operator sees network events (which cell or sector each phone is attached to, handovers, data sessions), not GPS; footfall products aggregate those events into grid cells and time windows, suppress any cell with fewer people than a threshold (k-anonymity), extrapolate from the operator's market share to the whole population with census weights, and sell counts, dwell times and origin–destination flows, never individual traces. Telefónica's Smart Steps, launched in the UK in 2012 for retailers' footfall questions, is exactly this product and the lineage of the resume's work. In the EU the ePrivacy rules allow operators to process location data only when it is anonymized or with consent, so the anonymization method is the product's license to exist. *App-SDK location data, the riskier source.* Most U.S. footfall panels come from location SDKs inside consumer apps; the FTC has acted repeatedly against brokers of that data — Kochava (sued in 2022), X-Mode/Outlogic and InMarket (orders in January 2024), Gravy Analytics with its Venntel subsidiary, and Mobilewalla (December 2024) — over selling visits to sensitive places such as clinics and places of worship. The lesson for a product: buy only aggregated or consented data, exclude sensitive locations at ingestion, keep the supplier's consent records, and expect buyers' legal teams to ask. Near's collapse, after a short life as a listed company, is the cautionary tale for a business built on brokered location data.

More names to know. *Telco AI and operations:* **Amdocs** (BSS/OSS with its amAIz GenAI platform for care and sales agents), **Ericsson** and **Nokia** (network-automation apps on the RAN Intelligent Controller and self-organizing-network software such as Nokia's MantaRay), **HPE Juniper Networking** (the Mist AI operations assistant; HPE completed the Juniper acquisition in 2025), **Mobileum** (roaming, fraud and network analytics), and the **AI-RAN Alliance** (operators and NVIDIA running AI workloads and radio processing on shared GPUs). *Location and geospatial:* **Foursquare** (places data, absorbed Placed and Factual), **Veraset** (mobility data, spun out of SafeGraph), **Esri** and **CARTO** (the GIS and spatial-analytics platforms where site-selection models are built and presented), **Mapbox** (maps and movement data). *How the telco-ops winners work:* closed-loop automation — anomaly detection on network key performance indicators, a root-cause model that maps alarms to the likely faulty element, a runbook-driven agent that proposes the fix, and an engineer who approves it — which is the same detect, diagnose, propose and approve loop as the SRE agents in chapter 23.9.

## 61.8 Conversational and voice AI

- **Sierra, Decagon, Intercom Fin, Ada** — LLM-native support agents with actions; Sierra sells outcomes-based pricing. *How:* deep integrations, evaluation loops, human handoff, enterprise security.
- **PolyAI, Parloa, Cresta, Observe.AI, Replicant, Smith.ai, Goodcall, Slang AI** — voice agents and contact-center AI from enterprise to SMB (the RingNest competitive set in chapter 58). *How:* telephony integration, deterministic rails for transactions, agent-assist and QA for humans.
- **Voice infrastructure — ElevenLabs Agents (renamed from Conversational AI in 2025), Vapi, Retell, Bland, LiveKit, Deepgram, Cartesia** — the picks-and-shovels of voice agents.
- **Drive-through — SoundHound (acquired SYNQ3), Presto, Hi Auto, and the chain-led programs (Wendy's with Google, Yum! Brands' platform)** — see chapter 41. Presto is also the cautionary case: in January 2025 it settled an SEC cease-and-desist order — an early "AI-washing" case, and one of the first brought against a public company rather than an investment adviser — over statements about its voice product that did not disclose how many orders needed human intervention; it was then sold to private investors and keeps operating. Disclose your human-in-the-loop share; it is a metric, not a secret.

**Critic's additions: how voice and support agents are priced and engineered, the rules, and more names.** *Pricing models, compared.* Per resolution (Intercom's Fin has charged $0.99 per resolved conversation), per outcome (Sierra prices on resolved tasks or saved cancellations), per minute for voice platforms (hosted platforms charge a platform fee per minute on top of model, speech and telephony costs), per seat for agent-assist. Outcome pricing only works when "resolved" is defined in the contract — no reopen within 72 hours, no escalation, customer did not call back — so define it before the pilot. *The latency budget for a voice turn* (target under about 800 ms from the caller's last word to the agent's first sound): endpointing and voice-activity detection 200–300 ms, final speech-to-text 100–300 ms, model time to first token 200–500 ms, text-to-speech first audio 100–200 ms; streaming every stage, a smaller model for routine turns, and speech-to-speech models that collapse the chain are how teams get under budget (chapter 41). *The rules.* The FCC ruled in February 2024 that AI-generated voices count as "artificial" voices under the TCPA, so outbound AI calls need prior express consent; call recording needs every party's consent in all-party-consent states such as California; several states require disclosure that a caller is talking to a bot in some settings (California's bot-disclosure law for sales and elections, Utah's AI disclosure law); and HIPAA applies the moment a clinic is the customer.

More names to know: **Cognigy** (enterprise conversational AI, acquired by NiCE for $955 million in 2025), **Kore.ai** and **Yellow.ai** (enterprise agent platforms with many channel integrations), **Uniphore** (conversation analytics and agents for contact centers), **ASAPP** (contact-center automation sold on cost per conversation), **Zendesk** (AI agents built on its 2024 acquisition of Ultimate), **Genesys**, **Five9** and **Talkdesk** (the contact-center platforms that bundle their own AI agents and decide which third parties get integrated), **Hyro** and **Assort Health** (voice agents specialized for healthcare scheduling and patient access). The structural point for a startup: the contact-center platforms own the telephony and the routing, so an independent agent must be either much better on one vertical's tasks (the RingNest thesis) or available inside their marketplaces.

## 61.9 Trading, markets and prediction markets

- **Kalshi, Polymarket** — the two prediction-market leaders (chapter 42); **Manifold, Metaculus** — forecasting communities; **Numerai** — crowdsourced models for a hedge fund; **Composer, QuantConnect** — strategy platforms for retail; **TopstepX/ProjectX, Apex** — prop-firm infrastructure that products like ScalpX (chapter 58) plug into.
- **Hebbia, Rogo, AlphaSense, Brightwave** — LLM research agents for finance; *How:* permission-aware retrieval over filings and internal documents with citations, workflow integration for analysts.

**Critic's additions: how prediction markets and retail trading platforms make money, where they are fragile, and more names.** *Prediction markets, mechanically.* A U.S. prediction market is a CFTC-regulated exchange (a designated contract market) listing event contracts that pay $1 if an outcome happens; the exchange earns trading fees, liquidity comes from professional market makers, and the hard engineering is in contract specification (unambiguous resolution criteria and sources), resolution itself (Kalshi resolves under its own rulebook; Polymarket's international venue uses UMA's optimistic oracle, where a proposed answer stands unless disputed), surveillance for insider and manipulative trading, and know-your-customer checks — the four places chapter 42 puts AI. Sports contracts brought Kalshi into litigation with state gaming regulators in 2025, with rulings that vary by state (verify the current position before quoting it). The field widened fast in 2025: Polymarket bought a CFTC-licensed exchange to return to U.S. users, DraftKings bought the licensed exchange Railbird, and FanDuel launched FanDuel Predicts with CME Group in December 2025. *Retail and prop trading, mechanically.* Prop-firm evaluation businesses (Topstep, Apex) sell evaluations in which traders pay to prove they can follow risk rules on simulated accounts; industry analyses suggest a large share of revenue comes from evaluation fees rather than trading, which is why rules on automation, news trading and copy trading are strict, and why a bot product's risk engine must encode each firm's rules exactly (chapter 58, idea F).

More names to know: **Robinhood** (a prediction-markets hub offered with Kalshi since 2025), **Interactive Brokers' ForecastEx** (its own event-contract exchange), **Crypto.com** (event contracts through its CFTC-registered derivatives exchange), **CME Group** (the incumbent exchange moving into event contracts with FanDuel), **DraftKings** (prediction markets after acquiring Railbird); for systematic retail trading, **NinjaTrader** (futures platform popular with prop traders, acquired by the crypto exchange Kraken in 2025 — not to be confused with Kraken Technologies in 61.5), **Alpaca** (an API-first broker built for algorithmic trading), **TradingView** (charting with Pine Script strategy backtesting, where many retail strategies are first written); for finance research, **Tegus** (expert-call transcripts, acquired by AlphaSense in 2024), **Kensho** (S&P Global's AI unit) and the research assistants inside **Bloomberg** and **FactSet** terminals, which set the bar any startup's citations must meet.

## 61.10 Patterns across the winners

1. **A system of record or a proprietary data loop** (ad events, claims photos, sensor streams, call recordings) feeds models that competitors cannot train.
2. **Workflow integration beats model quality**: the product lives where the work happens (Workfront, the POS, the EHR, the contact center).
3. **Deterministic rails plus generative flexibility** in every customer-facing agent.
4. **Evaluation and human review as features**, not afterthoughts, especially in regulated domains.
5. **Outcome-based or usage-based pricing** aligned with the customer's unit economics.
6. **Vertical focus first**, horizontal expansion later.

Use this list two ways: in interviews, name the competitor set and the mechanism when you discuss a use case; for startup ideas, find the gap each list leaves open (chapter 58's scoring sheet).

**Critic's additions: three more patterns, and the anti-patterns from companies that failed or were sanctioned.** Patterns the list above misses: (7) **distribution through someone else's system of record** — Kraken through utilities' customer bases, claims AI through the core-system and repair-shop networks, voice agents through contact-center marketplaces; the integration partner is the sales channel. (8) **a license or certification as the moat** — a CFTC exchange license, an insurance carrier license, HIPAA-ready operations, a SOC 2 report; slow to obtain, which is the point. (9) **data that improves across customers** — fraud graphs, repair-cost estimates and voice-quality models get better with every customer's data, if contracts allow that use; write the data-use clause into the first contract.

The anti-patterns, each with a verified case:

| Anti-pattern | Case | The lesson for an AI product |
|---|---|---|
| Overstating automation | Presto settled an SEC "AI-washing" cease-and-desist order in January 2025, one of the first against a public company; Builder.ai entered insolvency in May 2025 after its revenue figures were questioned, amid reports about how much of its "AI" work was done by people | Measure and disclose the human-in-the-loop share; investors, regulators and customers will ask |
| Building on brokered personal data | Near Intelligence filed for bankruptcy in December 2023 shortly after going public; the FTC's December 2024 actions against Gravy Analytics and Mobilewalla restricted the sale of sensitive-location data | Use first-party, consented or aggregated data; treat privacy as a product requirement |
| Novel hardware without a daily job to do | Humane's AI Pin was discontinued when HP bought the company's assets in early 2025 | Ship where the work already happens (pattern 2), not a new device the user must adopt |
| Healthcare automation without unit economics | Olive AI shut down in 2023; Babylon Health collapsed the same year | In regulated, integration-heavy markets, prove margin per customer before scaling sales |
| A thin layer over a model provider | Many 2023 writing and chat wrappers lost their market when model vendors and suites shipped the same feature | Own a workflow, an integration or a data loop the model vendor will not build |

When you discuss any company in an interview, name which pattern it relies on and which anti-pattern it is exposed to; that one sentence is what separates an insider from someone who read the press release.
