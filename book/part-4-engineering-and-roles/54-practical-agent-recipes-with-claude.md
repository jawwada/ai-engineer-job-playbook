# 54. Practical agent recipes with Claude: job applications, lead generation, motivated-seller finders, scrapers where browsers cannot go, and mixing determinism with non-determinism

> **What you need to be able to say:** how real, useful agents are built today with Claude Code / the Claude Agent SDK plus a few deterministic tools; where browser agents stop and scrapers start; how to keep the model's non-determinism inside a deterministic frame; and the legal and ethical lines (terms of service, personal data, outreach consent) that decide whether a recipe is a business or a liability.

## 54.1 The pattern behind every recipe

```
sources (portals, feeds, APIs, scrapers) → normalize (deterministic) → score/triage (model) →
act (deterministic tools with approvals) → track (database) → learn (evals, lessons)
```

Deterministic code owns ingestion, de-duplication, schemas, rate limits, caps, persistence and the final action; the model owns reading, judgement, writing and planning inside those rails. Claude Code (or a Deep Agents / Strands loop) is the orchestrator; skills hold the playbooks; MCP servers and scripts are the tools; a tracker table is the memory; an eval set keeps it honest.

**Critic's additions: the pre-flight check every recipe passes before it collects a single record.** The recipes below are only as legitimate as their inputs and outputs, and an agent multiplies both a good process and a bad one. Answer six questions in writing first (this is engineering guidance, not legal advice; for anything commercial, have counsel review the jurisdictions you operate in):

| Question | Acceptable answers | Stop signs |
|---|---|---|
| Are we allowed to collect from this source? | an official API or feed under its terms, a licensed data provider, your own accounts' exports, or public pages whose terms permit automated access | terms that prohibit scraping or bots, a login wall you would have to automate, a block or CAPTCHA you would have to defeat |
| Is any of it personal data? | none, or a documented lawful basis, the minimum fields, a retention period and a way to honour deletion and objection requests | "it's public, so it's free to use" (privacy law in the EU, UK and many other places still applies to public personal data) |
| How will we contact anyone? | channels and volumes that meet the outreach law of the recipient's country (54.3), with identification and a working opt-out | automated calls or texts to individuals, cold email where consent is required, messages that pretend to be personal |
| Does the agent decide anything about a person? | the agent ranks or drafts; a human decides | automated rejection or targeting of individuals in employment, housing, credit or insurance contexts |
| Does the platform allow automation of this action? | the platform's API, or a human clicking each submission | a browser agent acting inside a platform whose terms forbid automated activity |
| Can we show what happened? | a log of sources, evidence URLs, approvals and messages sent | no record of who approved what |

## 54.2 Recipe 1 — Applying for engineering jobs (Part 1 of this book)

Already built: alerts and searches → scout and score against a truth file → tailor resume and cover note → fill forms in the user's own browser with review → tracker → inbox triage. Determinism: caps, answer bank, the never-list, tracker updates; non-determinism: fit scoring, tailoring, replies. Where the browser cannot go (rate-limited boards, portals that detect automation), it does not go: it uses alerts, RSS/email feeds and official exports instead, and leaves submission to the human.

**Critic's additions: the rules that keep an application agent honest and within platform terms.** *Platform terms first:* LinkedIn's User Agreement prohibits bots, crawlers and browser plug-ins that scrape, automate messaging or otherwise automate activity, and many job boards have similar clauses; on those platforms the agent reads what you have saved or been sent (alerts, emails, exports), drafts answers and documents, and you perform every click and submission yourself. On an employer's own career site, the agent may pre-fill a form in your browser for you to review, and you submit. *Truthfulness:* every answer comes from the truth file — no invented experience, credentials, dates or work authorization, no tailoring that changes facts — because an application is a signed representation to an employer, and some employers ask whether AI was used, which you answer honestly. *Volume:* a daily cap and a fit threshold, so the agent sends you to the twenty roles that fit rather than spraying two hundred; recruiters notice and platforms throttle mass applicants. *Privacy:* the agent never enters sensitive data a legitimate application does not need (government identifiers, bank details, passwords), and treats any request for them as a scam signal (chapter 53.5). *Third parties:* contact details of recruiters or hiring managers found during research stay in your tracker for your own follow-up; they are not exported, enriched or added to any list.

## 54.3 Recipe 2 — B2B lead generation for a consultancy or product

**Goal.** A weekly list of 50 companies likely to need your service, with the right contact role, a reason, and a draft message you approve.

**Sources (legal first).** Your own CRM and inbox; public company pages, job postings (a company hiring "LLM engineer" is a lead for an AI consultancy), press releases, funding announcements, conference speaker lists, app-store and product changelogs, government tenders, review sites; data vendors with licensed B2B contact data (Apollo, ZoomInfo, Clay's providers) accessed through their APIs under their terms. Avoid scraping personal data from social networks against their terms; LinkedIn's agreement prohibits it and enforces it (the long hiQ v. LinkedIn litigation ended in 2022 with hiQ found in breach of the user agreement and bound by a permanent injunction, even though the appeals court had doubted that scraping public pages violated the US anti-hacking statute). Licensed data does not license the outreach: you remain responsible for having a lawful basis to use each contact, for telling people where their data came from when the law requires it (in the EU and UK, within a month of obtaining it and at the latest at first contact), and for honouring objections and deletion requests at once. Check what the vendor's own terms allow (most forbid resale and some forbid certain channels) and how it sourced the data.

**Pipeline.** Scheduled collectors (Apify actors for job boards and news sites whose terms permit it — many boards offer feeds or partner APIs instead — RSS, vendor APIs) → normalize to a `signals` table (company, signal type, evidence URL, date) → deterministic de-dup and enrichment (domain, size, industry from licensed data) → Claude scores fit against your ideal-customer profile with a rubric and writes a one-line "why now" with the evidence link → a human reviews the top 50 → Claude drafts outreach in your voice from a template library → you send (or your email tool sends after approval), respecting CAN-SPAM/GDPR/PECR (business-context, opt-out, no purchased consumer lists) → replies are triaged and the tracker learns which signals convert.

**Determinism/non-determinism.** Scoring rubric is a prompt, but thresholds, caps per day, suppression lists (existing customers, unsubscribed, competitors) and send actions are code. Evals: 100 past leads labeled won/lost → precision of the score at the top decile; a judge on message quality calibrated with your edits.

**Critic's additions: outreach law and deliverability, as rules the code enforces.** The rules differ by the *recipient's* country, so the lead record carries the country and the send step looks it up. The common cases (verify the current text for your market; not legal advice):

| Jurisdiction | Cold B2B email | What every message needs | Calls and texts |
|---|---|---|---|
| United States (CAN-SPAM) | allowed without prior consent | accurate sender and subject, identification as an advertisement where applicable, a postal address, a working opt-out honoured within 10 business days | the TCPA requires prior express written consent for marketing calls or texts to mobiles using an autodialer or an artificial or prerecorded voice; the FCC ruled in February 2024 that AI-generated voices count as artificial; respect the Do Not Call registry |
| Canada (CASL) | needs express or implied consent; implied consent covers, for example, an address conspicuously published for business purposes where the message is relevant to the person's role and no "no unsolicited email" notice is shown | sender identification, contact details, an unsubscribe mechanism | the same consent logic applies to texts |
| United Kingdom (PECR and UK GDPR) | allowed to corporate addresses without consent, but sole traders and some partnerships count as individuals and need consent | identify yourself, offer a simple opt-out, a legitimate-interests assessment, and a privacy notice | live marketing calls must be screened against the Telephone Preference Service and, for businesses, the Corporate TPS; automated marketing calls need prior consent |
| European Union (GDPR plus national ePrivacy laws) | varies by country: Germany's unfair-competition law requires prior consent for advertising email even to businesses; others allow relevant B2B email with an opt-out | a lawful basis (often legitimate interest with a documented balancing test), the source of the data, and an absolute right to object to direct marketing | national rules on calls vary; assume consent is needed for automated calls |

What the code enforces regardless: a suppression list checked immediately before every send (unsubscribes, objections, existing customers, competitors, countries you cannot email); a per-mailbox daily cap that starts small; one-click unsubscribe; no message the agent cannot stand behind factually (no invented mutual connections, "loved your recent post" when nobody read it, or fake urgency); and a log of what was sent, to whom, on what basis. For deliverability, Google's and Yahoo's sender requirements (since 2024) apply to anyone sending in volume to their users: SPF, DKIM and DMARC authentication, one-click unsubscribe processed within two days for bulk senders, and a spam-complaint rate kept under 0.3% (aim for under 0.1%). A complaint rate above that ends the campaign before any regulator notices.

## 54.4 Recipe 3 — "Motivated seller" finder for expensive cars (and similar scarce assets)

**Goal.** Surface listings of high-value cars whose sellers show signs of motivation (price drops, long time on market, relisting, "must sell", estate or relocation language, dealer trade-ins), so a buyer or broker can act first.

**Sources.** Marketplaces with public listings and, where offered, official APIs or data feeds (manufacturer certified-pre-owned feeds, auction-house results, dealer inventory feeds, classifieds whose terms permit automated access). Check each portal's terms: many prohibit scraping, some sell data access, some permit limited automated access to public pages. A robots.txt that does not disallow a path signals crawler preferences; it is not a licence, and the terms of service still govern. Apify and similar platforms host maintained scrapers ("actors") for common portals and handle proxies, rate limits and schema changes; the legal responsibility is still yours.

**Pipeline.** Daily collection of listings (make/model/year filters) → a `listings` table keyed by portal + listing id with price history → deterministic signals: price-drop count and size, days listed, relist detection (same VIN/photos), distance, dealer vs private → Claude reads the free-text for motivation language and condition details, extracts structured attributes (service history, accident mentions, modifications), and scores motivation with a rubric and evidence quotes → a daily digest ranked by score with the links → human contacts the seller through the platform's own messaging (no automated outreach; no scraping of phone numbers for cold calls — TCPA and local rules apply) → outcomes recorded (price achieved, response) to refine the rubric.

**Why this is a good agent problem.** The signals are mostly deterministic (price history, time on market); the model adds judgement from text and photos (a multimodal pass can flag inconsistencies between photos and description); the human closes. Evals: 200 historical listings with known outcomes (sold quickly, price cut later) → does the score predict negotiation room?

Same recipe applies to real estate (expired and long-listed properties; probate and foreclosure lists only within the limits below, which in practice rule them out for automation), machinery, boats, domain names, and B2B assets — always with the portal's terms and local privacy law checked first.

**Critic's additions: score the listing, not the person.** "Motivation" signals sit close to people's private circumstances, and the recipe must not turn into a tool for pressuring people in distress. The rules: use listing-level facts (price history, days listed, relists, the seller's own words in the ad) and do not infer or record life events about an identifiable seller (bereavement, divorce, illness, debt), even when the text hints at them — treat "estate sale" or "relocating" as attributes of the listing, never as a lever in negotiation; do not cross-reference a private seller with other sources to identify them, find a phone number or home address (no "skip tracing"); contact only through the platform's messaging, at human pace; in the EU and UK a private seller's listing is personal data, so keep the listing fields you need for the comparison, for a stated period, and delete the rest. For real estate, prefer price cuts and long time on market on portals that permit it; probate, foreclosure and divorce lists target people in hardship, often reach numbers on do-not-call registries, and in many places attract additional rules on solicitation, so they are out of scope for an automated pipeline. Fair-lending and fair-housing law also forbids targeting or excluding people by protected characteristics, which a model can do by proxy (neighbourhood, language) unless you test for it.

## 54.5 Recipe 4 — Using Apify and other scrapers where agents cannot go

Browser agents (Claude in Chrome, computer use) are slow, visible and sometimes blocked; they are right for logged-in, low-volume, high-judgement tasks on sites whose terms allow automated use of your account. For volume and coverage, use collection infrastructure:

- **Apify** — a marketplace of maintained scrapers ("actors") plus a platform for running your own (Crawlee for Node/Python), with scheduling, proxies, storage and webhooks; actors exist for job boards, marketplaces, maps, app stores, news and social platforms (where allowed) — and the existence of a ready-made actor for a site says nothing about whether that site permits it. Call it from the agent as a tool: start an actor with inputs, wait for the dataset, pull JSON.
- **Firecrawl / Jina Reader / Crawl4AI** — turn pages or whole sites into clean markdown for LLMs; good for documentation, company sites and news.
- **Browserbase / Playwright / Puppeteer** — headless browsers for sites that need JavaScript or logins you own; Playwright MCP servers let the agent drive them.
- **Bright Data / ScrapingBee / Zyte** — proxy and unblocking infrastructure (ethical use: public data, respect terms). Use them for reliability and geography on access you are permitted to have, never to defeat a block, a login wall, a CAPTCHA or a rate limit a site has put in front of you: a block is the site saying no.
- **Official APIs and feeds first** — RSS, sitemaps, public APIs, data partnerships, vendor exports: cheaper, stable, and legal.

**Rules.** Read robots.txt and terms; prefer APIs; rate-limit; cache; never collect personal data you do not have a lawful basis for; store evidence URLs; monitor for layout changes (a scraper that silently returns empty is the usual failure); keep collectors deterministic and separate from the model so a schema change does not become a hallucination.

**Critic's additions: where the legal lines run for collection (not legal advice).** Engineers are expected to know the shape of the risk, not to practise law. *Contract:* accepting terms (by creating an account or logging in) binds you, which is why logged-in scraping is the riskiest kind — hiQ lost on breach of LinkedIn's user agreement — while US courts have been more tolerant of logged-off collection of public pages (Meta's contract claim against Bright Data failed in 2024 for that reason). *Computer-misuse law:* in the US the Ninth Circuit doubted that public pages can be accessed "without authorization", but an earlier court treated continuing after an IP block and a cease-and-desist letter as unauthorized access (Craigslist v. 3Taps, 2013); the law is unsettled, so a block or a letter means stop. *Privacy:* in the EU and UK, data protection law applies to personal data whether or not it is public, including transparency duties toward people you never contacted; regulators have fined scrapers of public profiles and photos heavily (the Dutch authority fined Clearview AI €30.5 million in 2024). *Database and copyright rights:* in the EU, extracting a substantial part of a database built with substantial investment can infringe the database right, and copying text or images can infringe copyright regardless of how it was accessed. Engineering defaults that follow: identify your crawler with a contact address, honour crawl delays, collect the minimum fields, keep evidence of the terms at the time of collection, and run a data-protection impact assessment before any large-scale processing of personal data.

**Making the agent efficient with scrapers.** Let the deterministic collector produce a compact, normalized JSON (not raw HTML); have the model operate on diffs (new or changed listings) rather than the whole corpus; batch the model's scoring (the Batch API at half price for nightly runs); route easy classifications to a small model and reserve the frontier model for the top candidates; cache embeddings for de-dup; store everything so reruns cost nothing.

## 54.6 Recipe 5 — Determinism with non-determinism: the operating rules

1. **Deterministic frame, probabilistic core.** Schedules, schemas, caps, suppression lists, idempotent writes and final actions are code; reading, scoring, drafting and planning are the model.
2. **Structured outputs everywhere the model talks to code.** JSON schemas with validation; a repair step; never regex over prose.
3. **Temperature 0 and pinned models for scoring; sampling only for drafts.** Two caveats: many reasoning models accept only their default sampling settings (extended thinking, for example, does not combine with a custom temperature), and even temperature 0 is not bit-for-bit deterministic on hosted APIs, because batching and hardware change the arithmetic. Determinism therefore comes from pinned model snapshots, structured outputs, and caching each score keyed by input hash, prompt version and model version, so a rerun reuses the stored judgement unless you deliberately invalidate it.
4. **Thresholds and caps outside the prompt.** The model proposes a score; code decides what happens at 80 vs 60.
5. **Evidence with every judgement.** Quotes and URLs the human can check; no evidence, no action.
6. **Replayability.** Store inputs and outputs so a rerun with a new prompt can be compared on the same data (this is your eval set).
7. **Human approval on anything external** — messages, purchases, submissions — until a track record justifies automation, then automate the lowest-risk slice first. Some actions never graduate: where a platform's terms forbid automated activity, where the law requires consent you do not have, or where the action is a decision about a person (hiring, credit, housing), the human stays in the loop permanently.
8. **Budgets and alerts.** Cost per run, per day; stop on anomalies.

## 54.7 Twelve more recipes in one line each

Inbox triage with drafted replies (Gmail connector + rules); competitor and pricing monitoring (feeds, partner APIs, or scrapers where the site's terms permit + diff + model summaries); grant and tender scouting (government portals via official APIs + fit scoring); content repurposing (one long piece → posts, emails, slides with brand rules); customer-review mining (your own marketplace reviews via seller APIs or exports → themes, defects, feature requests); invoice and receipt bookkeeping (document AI + categorization + accountant review); meeting-notes-to-actions (transcript of a meeting recorded with consent → tasks in your tracker with owners); SEO content gap analysis (your Search Console data or a licensed search-data provider, not scraped result pages, + a crawl of your own site → briefs); recruiting sourcing from your own applicant pool (resume parsing + fit scoring, no scraping; a recruiter decides); research digests (arXiv/RSS → summaries + relevance for your field); property management (tenant emails → classified tickets, drafted responses, vendor dispatch with approval); sales-call coaching (transcripts of calls recorded with the required consent → rubric scores + coaching notes).

**Critic's additions: the three one-liners that are regulated activities.** *Recruiting:* a model that ranks or filters candidates is an automated employment decision tool in New York City (Local Law 144 requires an independent bias audit within the past year and notice to candidates before use) and a high-risk system under the EU AI Act (recruitment and candidate evaluation are listed uses, with obligations phasing in on a timetable the EU has been revising, so check the current date), and other jurisdictions are adding notice and anti-discrimination rules; keep the model to ranking and summarizing, keep a human decision on every rejection, log the reasons, and test the ranking for disparate impact by group before and after launch. *Call and meeting recording:* US federal law and most states require one party's consent, but around a dozen states, including California, require every party's consent, and in the EU and UK recording needs a lawful basis and clear notice; announce recording at the start of every call and let the coaching pipeline skip calls without it. *Search data:* Google's terms and robots.txt disallow automated querying of its result pages, so gap analysis should start from your own Search Console data or a licensed provider; scraping result pages is the fastest way to have a growth tool blocked.

**Interview line:** *"Useful agents are deterministic pipelines with a model in the middle: collectors and schemas and caps in code, judgement and language from Claude, evidence on every output, humans on every external action, and scrapers or APIs — not browser agents — wherever volume and coverage matter. And the terms of service decide what is a product and what is a lawsuit."*
