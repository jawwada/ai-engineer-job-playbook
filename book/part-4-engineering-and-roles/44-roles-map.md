# 44. The roles map: AI Engineer, Forward Deployed Engineer, ML Engineer, MLOps, DevOps/Platform, Data Engineer, FinOps, SRE, Solutions Architect, Data Scientist, Quant AI Engineer

> **The idea:** titles overlap, but each role owns a different failure. Knowing what each role does, what it must know, and how it works with the others lets you read a JD correctly (chapter 35), position your resume honestly (chapter 7), and answer "how would you work with the platform team?" with specifics. Each role below has: what they own, a typical week, the knowledge required, the interview focus, adjacent roles, and three example use cases.

**Adjacent titles you will meet in JDs.** *Research Engineer* (implements and scales research code; interview is coding plus paper discussion), *Evaluation Engineer / Model Quality Engineer* (owns eval sets, judges and quality dashboards; a growing title in 2026), *Prompt Engineer* (declining as a standalone title; the work moved into AI engineering and evaluation), *AI Solutions Engineer / Sales Engineer* (pre-sales demos and POCs; closer to FDE but without owning production), *Developer Advocate* (content, samples, community; judged on adoption), *Member of Technical Staff* (lab-style generic title; read the team description, not the title), *Staff/Principal* prefixes (scope across teams and the expectation that you write the ADRs others follow). *Analytics Engineer* and *Security Engineer* are in the table above because AI teams increasingly hire both.

## 44.2 AI Engineer (Applied AI, LLM Engineer, GenAI Engineer)

**Owns.** Features and agents built on models: prompts, tools, retrieval, orchestration, structured outputs, guardrails, evals, cost and latency — integrated into product code. **Typical week.** Iterating on an agent's tool design against a failing eval slice; adding a reranker and measuring recall; wiring OTel traces; a design review for a new use case; a cost review; on-call for the LLM gateway. **Must know.** Chapters 14–34 of this book; production Python (chapter 49); one cloud deeply; API design; testing and CI. **Adjacent.** ML engineers (models), data engineers (corpora), platform (runtime), product (acceptance criteria). **Use cases.** Policy assistant with permissions (chapter 40 A); support agent with actions (40 B); document extraction (40 D).

## 44.3 Forward Deployed Engineer

**Owns.** A customer's outcome: discovery, scoping, building the first use case in the customer's environment, security and compliance review, production go-live, enablement of the customer's team, and the feedback loop to the product. **Typical week.** Two days on-site or in customer calls (discovery, demos, workshops), two days building (integration, data access, evals), one day on security questionnaires, documentation and internal product feedback. **Must know.** AI-engineer skills plus: enterprise integration (SSO, VPC, IAM, APIs without docs), data access negotiation, security reviews (SOC 2, HIPAA, data residency), writing acceptance criteria, presenting to executives, handling ambiguity and politics, travel tolerance. **Interview focus.** Use-case walkthroughs (chapter 36), design under constraints, a presentation, behavioural stories about customers. **Use cases.** Deploying a vendor's agent platform inside a bank's VPC with their identity provider; building a claims triage POC for an insurer and taking it to production; standing up a RAG assistant on a hospital's SharePoint with PHI controls; running a two-day workshop to turn a utility's ten ideas into three scoped use cases with evals.

## 44.4 ML Engineer

**Owns.** Training and serving models: data prep and features, model development, offline and online evaluation, serving infrastructure, retraining, monitoring. **Must know.** PyTorch/TensorFlow, scikit-learn, gradient boosting, deep learning for the domain (vision, NLP, recommenders), feature stores, model serving (TorchServe/Triton/vLLM/SageMaker/Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform endpoints — the platform Google called Vertex AI until April 2026), experiment tracking, A/B testing. **Interview focus.** ML fundamentals, coding, a modeling case. **Use cases.** Two-tower ranking at 10K QPS (chapter 36.3); demand forecasting for inventory (36.4); segmentation models for drone inspection (36.5); fine-tuning a small LLM for classification (chapter 26).

## 44.5 MLOps / LLMOps Engineer

**Owns.** The machinery: training pipelines (Kubeflow, Agent Platform Pipelines — formerly Vertex AI Pipelines — SageMaker Pipelines, Databricks Lakeflow Jobs, Airflow), experiment tracking and model registry (MLflow, W&B, Unity Catalog), feature stores, serving platforms and autoscaling, monitoring (drift, performance, data quality), CI/CD for models and prompts, evaluation infrastructure, cost. **LLMOps additions.** Prompt registries and versioning, eval datasets and judges in CI, gateways with routing and caching, tracing with GenAI conventions, guardrail services, RAG index refresh pipelines. **Must know.** Kubernetes and containers, CI/CD, IaC, Python, the cloud's ML platform, observability, security basics. **Interview focus.** Design a training/serving platform; debug a failing pipeline; write infra code. **Use cases.** A retraining pipeline with validation gates and canary rollout for the ranking model; a prompt-and-eval CI gate for the marketing reviewer agents; an index refresh pipeline with ACL sync for the policy assistant; cost dashboards per feature from OTel token attributes.

## 44.6 DevOps / Platform Engineer

**Owns.** The paved road for all engineers: source control workflows, CI/CD pipelines, artifact registries, infrastructure as code, Kubernetes clusters or serverless platforms, environments, secrets, networking, identity, developer portals and golden templates — plus the GPU and model-serving infrastructure in AI-heavy organizations. **Must know.** Chapter 45. **Interview focus.** Design a deployment pipeline; troubleshoot a broken cluster; write Terraform; explain blue/green vs canary. **Use cases.** GitOps with Argo CD for forty microservices; a GPU node pool with autoscaling for model serving; a secure Bedrock access path with VPC endpoints and IAM; an internal template that stands up a new agent service with tracing and eval gates in an hour.

## 44.7 SRE

**Owns.** Reliability as an engineering discipline: SLIs/SLOs and error budgets, incident response and post-mortems, capacity planning, performance, toil automation, on-call health. **Must know.** Observability stacks, distributed systems failure modes, load testing, chaos engineering basics, cloud and Kubernetes operations, scripting. **Interview focus.** Incident scenarios ("latency doubled at 9 am"), SLO design, system design for reliability. **AI additions.** Provider rate limits and outages (fallback models), GPU saturation, cost as a reliability signal, quality SLOs via judge sampling. **Use cases.** An SLO for the support agent (p95 < 3 s, 99.5% availability, faithfulness ≥ 0.95 sampled); a runbook for provider outages with automatic model fallback; capacity planning for a product launch in tokens per minute.

## 44.8 Data Engineer

**Owns.** Ingestion, transformation, modeling and serving of data: batch and streaming pipelines, lakehouse/warehouse tables, data quality, lineage, cost; increasingly the document and embedding pipelines that feed RAG. **Must know.** Chapter 48. **Interview focus.** SQL and Spark problems, pipeline design, data modeling. **Use cases.** Medallion pipeline from five retail data sources into a unified model (36.4); streaming features for ad ranking (36.3); a document pipeline that parses, chunks, embeds and indexes 2M files with ACLs (40 A); CDC from an ERP into the lakehouse for an agent's tools.

## 44.9 Data Scientist / Applied Scientist

**Owns.** Turning questions into analyses, experiments and models; measurement and causal reasoning; communicating decisions. **Must know.** Statistics and experiment design, causal inference and uplift (Part 6), ML modeling, SQL, visualization, domain context. **Interview focus.** Case studies, statistics, modeling, communication. **AI additions.** Designing evals as experiments, judge calibration, offline/online metric alignment. **Use cases.** Promotional-lift analysis for e-commerce; churn and CLV models with actuaries (36.6); footfall analytics for retail locations; A/B gates for ranking launches.

## 44.10 FinOps Practitioner / Cloud Cost Engineer

**Owns.** Visibility (tagging, allocation, showback/chargeback, unit economics), optimization (rightsizing, scheduling, storage tiers, commitments, architecture changes), and operation (budgets, anomaly alerts, forecasting, governance) of cloud and AI spend — in partnership with engineering and finance. **Must know.** Chapter 47. **Interview focus.** A cost case ("spend doubled; find why and fix it"), commitment strategy, stakeholder scenarios. **Use cases.** Attributing LLM spend per feature with OTel attributes and cutting 69% (chapter 31); a 25% pipeline cost reduction via scheduling and rightsizing (36.5); Kubernetes cost allocation per team; a commitment plan for steady GPU workloads.

## 44.11 Solutions Architect (AI/Cloud)

**Owns.** Target architectures, reference patterns, build-vs-buy, vendor and model selection, security and compliance alignment, and the review process that keeps thirty teams consistent. **Must know.** Breadth across chapters 14–34 and 45–50; the ability to draw and defend an architecture in front of executives and engineers. **Interview focus.** Architecture review of a proposed design; trade-off discussions; presentation. **Use cases.** The enterprise agent platform (40 F); a multi-cloud model strategy behind a gateway; a migration from a classic Dialogflow ES bot to a hybrid agent (deterministic flows kept as flow-based agents, an LLM agent for open dialogue, both in CX Agent Studio, the successor to Dialogflow CX); a build-vs-buy review that keeps a managed RAG service for the HR assistant but builds custom retrieval for the legal corpus because the eval set showed a 15-point recall gap on jargon.

## 44.12 How the roles fit together on one project

Take the policy assistant (40 A): the **data engineer** builds the document pipeline with ACLs; the **AI engineer** builds retrieval, prompts, agent and evals; the **MLOps engineer** wires the eval gate, tracing and index refresh; the **platform engineer** provides the runtime, gateway and CI/CD; the **SRE** sets SLOs and runs incidents; the **FinOps practitioner** attributes and trends cost; the **architect** approves the design and the vendor choices; the **data scientist** designs the adoption experiment; the **FDE** does all of it inside the customer's environment with a smaller team and tells the customer's executives what they got. Knowing which hat you are wearing in each conversation is half of being senior.

**The same project under incident: who does what, in what order.** Interviewers who ask "how would you work with the platform team?" are really asking whether you know the handoffs. Walk them through an incident.

*Tuesday 09:12.* The nightly index refresh for the policy assistant shipped 1,800 new chunks without ACL principals because the SharePoint connector's permission field was renamed. At 09:40 an engineer asks about parental-leave policy and sees a quote from a restricted HR document.

1. **SRE** (09:41) receives the guardrail alert ("citation to document with empty ACL list"), declares a Sev-2 under the quality SLO ("0 permission leaks per month" is a hard SLO, not an error-budget one), and pages the on-call AI engineer. Artifact: incident channel, timeline, status page entry for the internal tool.
2. **AI engineer** (09:50) flips the feature flag that disables retrieval for documents indexed after 02:00, verifies with the negative test ("engineer asks about HR-restricted content, expects abstention"), and keeps the assistant up in degraded mode. Artifact: flag change, trace ids of the leaked responses.
3. **Data engineer** (10:05) traces the chunk rows to the connector run, finds the renamed field, adds a contract test ("ACL list must be non-empty for sources that have permissions"), quarantines the 1,800 rows, and reruns the refresh. Artifact: data-contract change, quarantine table, backfill run id.
4. **MLOps engineer** (10:30) adds the negative test to the CI eval gate and makes the index refresh pipeline fail closed when the ACL coverage of a run drops below 100% of documents from permissioned sources. Artifact: pipeline gate, new eval cases.
5. **Platform engineer** (11:00) confirms that the connector's service account could read the restricted library only because of a broad site-level grant and narrows it. Artifact: IAM change in IaC, policy-as-code rule.
6. **Security engineer** (same day) assesses exposure (12 users saw 3 restricted passages), triggers the data-protection notification process if required, and adds the case to the red-team suite.
7. **FinOps** notes the reindex cost (an extra 40 million embedding tokens) and that the refresh pipeline now runs with a cap; **data scientist** checks whether the adoption experiment's metrics were contaminated by the degraded hours; **architect** records the ADR: "ACL filtering is enforced in the retrieval service from the document table, never from the index alone"; **FDE** tells the customer's CISO what happened, what changed, and what evidence shows it cannot recur.

Post-mortem action items have owners from six roles, and the eval set grew by four cases. That is what "working well with the platform team" looks like in practice; say it with this level of detail and the interviewer stops probing.

## 44.13 Quant AI Engineer: the role, and the trading knowledge it requires

**Owns.** Models and systems that turn data into trading decisions and survive contact with real markets: alpha research pipelines, point-in-time data and feature stores, backtesters with realistic costs, execution and risk models, and, since 2025, the LLM and agent tooling that reads filings, calls and news or that writes and tests research code. **Must know.** Everything the ML engineer knows, plus market structure, the strategy families, backtesting hygiene, portfolio and risk mathematics, execution, and the compliance rules that bound all of it. **Interview focus.** Probability and statistics, coding under time pressure, a modeling case with a trap in it, market knowledge, and a research presentation defended against a sceptical desk. **AI additions.** NLP over unstructured financial text, research agents with leakage guardrails, and model-risk documentation. **Use cases.** Earnings-call and filing signals for a mid-frequency equity strategy; alternative-data ingestion with point-in-time versioning; a research assistant that proposes, backtests and ranks hypotheses under a pre-registered protocol; execution-cost models for an options market maker.

### 44.13.1 Where the role sits

The title varies more than the work. Hedge funds and asset managers say *quant researcher* (finds signals), *quant developer* (builds the platform) and, increasingly, *ML researcher* or *AI engineer, quant* (the overlap this section is about). Proprietary trading firms and market makers (Jane Street, Citadel Securities, Hudson River Trading, Jump, Optiver, IMC and their peers) run the same split at lower latency, and crypto venues run it with thinner data and looser rules. Across all of them the role owns three failures that no other role on the roles map owns: a backtest that lies, a model that saw the future, and a system that is right too late. Everything in the knowledge list below exists to prevent one of the three.

The frequency of the strategy decides the engineering. High-frequency and market-making work lives in C++, FPGAs and co-located servers, where a microsecond matters and machine learning is small and offline; mid-frequency work (holding periods of minutes to days) is where Python, feature stores and most ML live; low-frequency and fundamental work (weeks to quarters) is where LLM reading of text and alternative data pays most. An AI engineer entering the field almost always enters at mid or low frequency, and should say so.

### 44.13.2 Trading knowledge requirements

The list is long because interviewers probe breadth before depth. The first column is what a mid-frequency quant AI engineer must be able to explain without notes; the second is what you should recognise and be able to look up; the third is what earns extra credit.

| Area | Must know | Should know | Extra credit |

A sentence that signals you understand the whole table: *an edge is a small, decaying statistical advantage that costs, impact, risk limits and regulation each take a bite from, so the job is to measure it honestly before and after each bite.*

### 44.13.3 Machine learning in markets: what is different

Financial data breaks the assumptions most ML training assumes, and the interview checks that you know which ones.

- **Signal-to-noise is very low.** A daily equity return is mostly noise; a model that explains 1% of its variance can be very profitable, and a model that reports 60% accuracy on a direction task has almost certainly leaked. Expect small effects, large samples and heavy ensembling.
- **Observations are not independent.** Labels built from overlapping windows share information, so random k-fold cross-validation leaks the test set into training. The remedy is **purged cross-validation**: drop training observations whose label windows overlap the test fold, and add an **embargo** period after each test fold so serial correlation cannot carry information back. Walk-forward evaluation (train on the past, test on the next period, roll) is the simpler version and the one you describe first.
- **Labels are a design choice.** The fixed-horizon return is a poor label because volatility varies; the **triple-barrier** method labels each observation by which of a profit-take, stop-loss or time barrier is hit first, which matches how a position is actually managed. **Meta-labeling** trains a second model on whether to act on the first model's signal and at what size, separating "which side" from "how much".
- **Everything is non-stationary.** Relationships change with regimes, crowding and the strategy's own trading. Models are retrained on schedules, monitored for feature drift and return attribution, and retired when the edge decays; a model's half-life is a number the desk expects you to estimate.
- **Multiple testing is the central sin.** Trying a hundred features and reporting the best backtest guarantees an inflated result. Bailey, López de Prado and co-authors quantified this as the deflated Sharpe ratio and the probability of backtest overfitting; the practical rules are to pre-register hypotheses, count every trial, hold out a final period that nobody touches until the end, and prefer economic rationale over discovered patterns.
- **Costs decide.** A signal with a gross Sharpe of 2 and a turnover of 300% a year can be worthless after spread, impact and borrow. Every backtest is reported net of a cost model, and the cost model is validated against the firm's real fills.
- **Where LLMs fit, and the trap they bring.** The productive uses in 2026 are reading: earnings-call tone and guidance changes, risk-factor sections in filings, event extraction from news, entity resolution across messy sources, and research agents that draft code, run backtests and rank hypotheses under the pre-registration protocol above. The trap is **knowledge leakage**: a model pretrained on text through 2025 "knows" what happened to companies in 2024, so any evaluation on history must use a model with a training cut-off before the test period, or a point-in-time text corpus with the model used only as a feature extractor whose features are checked for lookahead. A second trap is that LLM features are expensive per token (chapter 63) and the signal in text decays within hours for news and within days for filings, so the pipeline's latency budget is part of the research question.

### 44.13.4 The systems a quant AI engineer builds

- **Point-in-time data platform.** Every dataset versioned by when it was known, not when it was true; fundamentals with revision history; a universe table that records membership by date so survivorship bias is impossible by construction; corporate-action adjustment applied at read time with the adjustment date recorded.
- **Feature store and research environment.** Features computed once with their as-of timestamps, shared between research and production so the same code produces both (the training-serving skew problem of chapter 40b, with money on the line); notebooks that cannot see the hold-out period; experiment tracking that counts trials.
- **Backtester.** Event-driven or vectorised, with a fill model (queue position for limit orders, participation-rate impact for market orders), a cost model, borrow availability for shorts, and risk limits applied as they would be live. The number it reports is net Sharpe with its confidence interval, maximum drawdown, turnover, capacity and the count of trials behind it.
- **Execution and risk engines.** Order management, pre-trade risk checks (position limits, fat-finger limits, restricted lists), execution algorithms with arrival-price benchmarks, real-time exposure and PnL attribution by factor and by signal.
- **Model-risk and monitoring.** A model inventory with documentation, validation reports, ongoing monitoring of feature drift, prediction drift and realised versus expected returns, and a retirement rule. For LLM components, the evaluation and judge discipline of chapter 66 applied to extraction accuracy, with human audit of a sample.
- **Latency tiers.** Batch research in Python; intraday signals in services with sub-second budgets; anything faster handed to the C++ team with a model exported to a small, fixed-cost form. Knowing where the boundary is, and not promising ML where the latency budget cannot afford it, is part of the role.

### 44.13.5 The interview

A quant loop usually has five parts, and an AI engineer's background covers two of them well and three of them only with preparation.

1. **Probability and statistics**, often as puzzles: expected values, conditional probability, Bayes, variance of sums, properties of estimators, hypothesis tests and their power, regression assumptions. Practice until the standard ones are reflexes.
2. **Coding**: chapter 39d's patterns, plus numerical care (floating point, vectorisation, time-series operations without lookahead) and the ability to write a clean backtest loop on a whiteboard.
3. **Modeling case** with a trap: a dataset, a question, and a leak or a cost you must find. Say your validation scheme before your model.
4. **Market knowledge**: the table in 44.13.2 at "must know" depth, with a current-events question that tests whether you follow markets at all.
5. **Research presentation**: a project defended to people whose job is to find the hole. Bring the trial count, the cost model and the out-of-sample result, and name the weakness before they do.

Questions you should expect, with the shape of a good answer:

- *"Your backtest shows a Sharpe of 3. What do you check first?"* Lookahead in the data (as-of timestamps, adjustment dates), the universe (survivorship), the cost model (is it net?), the number of trials behind the result, and whether the out-of-sample period was ever touched. A Sharpe of 3 on daily equity data is a bug until proven otherwise.
- *"Why not random k-fold cross-validation on returns?"* Overlapping labels and serial correlation leak the test set; use walk-forward or purged cross-validation with an embargo.
- *"How would you size a position from a signal?"* Kelly gives the growth-optimal fraction as expected return over variance; nobody runs full Kelly because the estimates are noisy and the drawdowns are intolerable, so a fraction of it, capped by risk limits and liquidity.
- *"Explain implementation shortfall."* The difference between the price when the decision was made and the price achieved, decomposed into delay, impact and the opportunity cost of unfilled quantity; the benchmark that execution algorithms are judged against.
- *"How would you use an LLM in a trading strategy?"* As a feature extractor over text with a point-in-time corpus and a model whose training cut-off precedes the test period, evaluated for leakage, with the signal's decay measured; not as the decision maker.
- *"What would make you retire a model?"* Realised returns outside the expected band for a defined window after attribution rules out execution and costs, feature drift beyond thresholds, or a documented change in the mechanism the edge relied on.
- *"What is insider trading, and how does your pipeline avoid it?"* Trading on material non-public information; the pipeline ingests only licensed or public data, logs provenance, enforces information barriers, and compliance reviews new data sources before they are used.

**Pitfalls that end the loop.** Quoting a gross Sharpe; a backtest without a cost model or a trial count; "the model is 70% accurate"; using future-adjusted prices; an LLM evaluated on history it was trained on; promising sub-millisecond ML; describing a strategy from a book as if it were a current edge; and, in the market-knowledge round, not knowing what the spread on a liquid large-cap equity is (a few basis points) or what a basis point is.

### 44.13.6 Getting in from AI engineering, and what to put on the resume

The transferable assets are real: production ML, evaluation discipline, data pipelines, cost control and the LLM work that quant teams are now hiring for. The gaps are market knowledge and backtesting hygiene, and both are learnable in months. Read Harris for market structure, López de Prado for the ML specifics (purging, triple barrier, meta-labeling, the deflated Sharpe ratio) and Grinold and Kahn for portfolio mathematics; build one honest strategy end to end on free daily data with a cost model and a trial count, and write it up with the weakness stated first. Resume lines that land: *"Built a point-in-time earnings-call feature pipeline (12k calls, as-of versioned) and evaluated it with purged walk-forward CV; signal decayed within three days; net Sharpe 0.6 after a 5 bp cost model over 2019–2025 out-of-sample, 14 trials logged."* The numbers are modest, and that is the point: a quant desk trusts modest numbers reported with their costs and their trial count, and distrusts everything else. Chapter 42 covers the adjacent prediction-markets roles, where the same statistics apply and trading strategies are explicitly out of scope.
